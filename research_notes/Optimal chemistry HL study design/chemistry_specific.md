# Chemistry-specific learning research and IB Chemistry HL exam evidence

Reading depth, because it affects how much weight each claim can bear:
- **[A]** = I read the published abstract only (retrieved through the OpenAlex or publisher record). Most journal full texts returned 403 errors or unreadable PDFs.
- **[S]** = search-result excerpt or secondary description only. Verify before relying on it.
- **[L]** = primary IB document in this project: exam cover pages, or examiner reports in `data/chunks/subject_reports_2001_2025.jsonl`.
- No item here was read in full text, so effect sizes are often missing from the sources I could reach. Where none is given, read that as "not available to me", not "small".

## Chemistry problem solving: Bodner & Herron, Gabel, Johnstone, working memory, expert vs novice categorisation

### Takeaway
Well established as description: novices in chemistry sort and represent by surface features and overload on multi-step, multi-representation tasks. Suggestive only: that sorting or categorising tasks train expertise, since nearly all the chemistry work uses sorting as a measure, not an intervention, and the physics replication complicates the clean expert/novice split.

### Cited Findings
- Exercise vs problem: the distinction is familiarity, not difficulty. If you know what to do on reading it, it is an exercise, and a solved problem becomes an exercise for that solver [S] — [Bodner group chapter, Purdue](https://chemed.chem.purdue.edu/chemed/bodnergroup/PDF_2008/73%20Treagust%20chpt.pdf) (attribution of the excerpt to this exact PDF is not confirmed; the PDF text was unreadable through my tools). The search describes this as drawing on Hayes's definition.
- Algorithms plus prior knowledge may be what turns problems into exercises [S] — [Bodner group materials](https://chemed.chem.purdue.edu/chemed/bodnergroup/PDF_2008/81%20Gautam%20Educ%20Quimica.pdf) (same attribution caveat).
- Gabel (1999) is a review-level statement: chemistry's complexity is shown by 15 years of problem-solving and misconception research, with hope placed in relevance and collaborative programmes. The abstract gives no intervention effect [A] — [Gabel 1999, J Chem Educ](https://doi.org/10.1021/ed076p548).
- Johnstone (1991) relates science-learning difficulty to the nature of science and to teaching that ignores how children learn, and proposes an information-processing model. This is theory, not an experiment [A] — [Johnstone 1991](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2729.1991.tb00230.x).
- The triple-representation idea is widely used but has no canonical form. Taber (2013) notes it has been reconceptualised in diverse ways; Talanquer (2011) says the adaptations can cause confusion and proposes a broader structure of chemical knowledge. Treat the triangle as a useful heuristic, not a tested result [A] — [Taber 2013](https://doi.org/10.1039/c3rp00012e); [Talanquer 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/09500690903386435).
- Working-memory overload hypothesis (Johnstone & El-Banna): a solver fails if a problem's M-demand exceeds their M-capacity. Tsaparlis's test found the model succeeded in some cases and failed in others, even at an M-demand of 2 [A] — [Tsaparlis 1998, IJSE](https://doi.org/10.1080/0950069980200306).
- Stamovlasis & Tsaparlis (2012): high-school students on organic-synthesis (non-algorithmic) problems, demand M = 3 to M = 8. A cusp catastrophe model fitted better (R² = .73–.84) than the linear model (R² = .52–.66), with working memory as asymmetry and field dependence and logical thinking as bifurcation factors. This means performance can fall abruptly rather than gradually as demand rises. It is a model-fit result, not an intervention [A] — [Sci Educ 2012](https://doi.org/10.1002/sce.21002).
- Across nine studies with a combined 250 first-year undergraduates, scientific reasoning showed no correlation with performance, working memory capacity a weak one, and functional M-capacity and field independence a strong role [S] — [ERIC EJ721304](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ721304) (attribution of the excerpt to this record is not confirmed). This conflicts with Cracolice et al. 2008 (next section), where reasoning skill did predict conceptual success.
- Chi et al. (1981, physics): experts begin from principles, novices from literal problem features [A] — [Chi, Feltovich & Glaser](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0502_2).
- Mason & Singh (2011, physics; three introductory classes of more than 100 students each): large overlap between calculus-based introductory students and graduate students on "good" categorisations. They say this contrasts with Chi et al. and suggests a wide spread of expertise, not two clean groups [A] — [Mason & Singh 2011](https://doi.org/10.1103/physrevstper.7.020110).
- Chemistry card sort (Krieter et al. 2016): faculty sort by deep fundamental ideas, novices by surface features such as presentation or vocabulary. The task distinguishes the populations and is proposed for tracking progress [A] — [Krieter et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jchemed.5b00992).
- Irby et al. (2016) card sort on the three representation levels: on average participants moved from sorting by level of representation towards sorting by underlying principle, but graduate students were an exception and sorted mainly by representation level [S] — [Irby et al. 2016, CERP](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2016/rp/c5rp00150a). Machine-learning reanalysis found novice-like sorts produced more categories than the task's built-in ones [S] — [Sizemore et al. 2024](https://www.doi.org/10.1039/D2RP00029F).
- Yuriev et al. (2017) "Goldilocks Help": a workflow with explicit phases and prompts aimed at dead ends and false starts. Over two years it shifted students' belief in their ability to use strategies and showed they could use them. No control-group effect size in the abstract [A] — [Yuriev et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1039/c7rp00009j). Later first-year work found structured solving and explicit reasoning went with success, while monitoring and evaluation stayed weak in written work [S] — [CERP 2024](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2024/rp/d3rp00284e).

### Inferences
- Organising questions only by syllabus code is the novice's organisation. An additional tag by principle (for R1.2: same state-function logic across bond enthalpy, ΔHf, ΔHc and Born-Haber) would let the student see the deep structure. The research supports this as a description of expertise, not as a proven training method.
- A sort-the-questions exercise would be cheap to try once as a self-diagnostic of surface-feature thinking. I would not expect a measured gain from it.
- The overload evidence argues for keeping working memory free in multi-step energetics: writing down the cycle and intermediate values, not holding them mentally. This is my inference from the model; no study here tests it in energetics.

### Gaps
- I could not read Bodner & Herron (2002) or Gabel's stoichiometry and problem-solving papers directly, so no numbers are reported for them.
- The Johnstone & El-Banna (1986) original did not resolve in my searches; it is known here only through Tsaparlis's description.
- I found no Lowery Bretz card-sort paper. The chemistry sort papers found are Irby et al. and Krieter et al. Lowery Bretz's relevant work found here is on reaction coordinate diagrams (see the representations section). Check whether the brief's attribution mixes these up.
- I found no trial showing that practising categorisation improves chemistry exam scores.

## Algorithmic vs conceptual understanding, and how to teach procedures without inert algorithms

### Takeaway
Well established and replicated: students solve chemistry calculations more often than they answer paired conceptual questions. How to close the gap is much less settled; the best chemistry evidence here favours eliciting and integrating students' ideas with explanation, and one quasi-experimental worked-example study suggests self-explanation prompts are the active part.

### Cited Findings
- Nurrenbern & Pickering (1987) tested the assumption that solving problems equals understanding molecular concepts [A] — [J Chem Educ 1987](https://doi.org/10.1021/ed064p508). Secondary summaries report that about two-thirds of students who could do gas-law problems failed the particulate gas question [S]; I did not see the original table. The particulate question's validity was later tested and choosing a wrong state of matter did not look like a major threat [S] — [J Chem Educ 2013 record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_1415375439).
- Cracolice, Deming & Ehlert (2008), n = 94, first-term general chemistry; paired questions on density, stoichiometry, gas laws, molarity. Algorithmic success always exceeded conceptual success. Better reasoners outscored poorer reasoners on all question types, significantly on 3 of 4 conceptual questions and on the ACS final. The authors read reasoning skill as one cause of the gap [A] — [Cracolice et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1021/ed085p873).
- Nakhleh (1993) frames students along a spectrum from algorithmic solvers to conceptual thinkers, using assessment questions to show it [A] — [Nakhleh 1993](https://doi.org/10.1021/ed070p52). A secondary source reports that across first-year levels from remedial to honours, conceptual ability lagged far behind algorithmic ability (sample described as 60 freshmen) [S] — [Nakhleh & Mitchell 1993 record](https://doi.org/10.1021/ed070p190).
- Replication at national-exam level exists but was not read — [CERP 2005](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2005/rp/b2rp90001g).
- Teichert & Stacy (2002), college general chemistry: discussion sections that elicited students' initial conceptions on bond energy and spontaneity and integrated them through explanation beat a control group on a written midterm, the thermodynamics portion of an ACS exam and interviews. Control students contradicted themselves more on bond energy. n and effect size not in the abstract. This is the closest intervention to the student's next topic [A] — [Teichert & Stacy 2002](https://doi.org/10.1002/tea.10033).
- Cooper et al. (2010): even with effort spent on "foolproof" rules, students at all levels struggle to draw Lewis structures and often do not connect the skill to why they learn it. The authors argue conventional procedure teaching conflicts with how people learn. This is mixed-methods description, plus suggestions [A] — [Cooper et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1021/ed900004y).
- Hoban et al. (2012, two Dublin undergraduate samples): maths difficulty in kinetics and thermodynamics may come from insufficient mathematical understanding, not from a failure to transfer [A] — [Hoban et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1080/0020739x.2012.690895).
- IB examiners say the same in their own terms: in May 2025 Paper 1A TZ2 they advised novel questions so that students apply concepts instead of memorising set procedures or answers, and varied question formats for the same concept. May 2024 Paper 2 advised avoiding rote explanations that may not answer the question [L] — [subject reports, 25M 1A HL TZ2 and 24M P2 HL recommendations](file:///C:/Users/Arturo/Documents/ib_tee/data/chunks/subject_reports_2001_2025.jsonl).

### Inferences
- Every R1.1 and R1.2 calculation type should have a paired conceptual question, since calculation success is poor evidence of understanding. A "Mastered" tick earned from calculations alone would overstate mastery. Plausible next pairs: why ΔH is negative in terms of bonds; why a value calculated from average bond enthalpies differs from an experimental one; what a calorimetry temperature rise means for system and surroundings.
- Pure drill should not be condemned: the algorithmic skill is itself rewarded by IB calculation marks. The evidence warns against treating it as sufficient, not against practising it.
- Verbal justification should follow the procedure step (why this sign, why this mass), not replace it.

### Gaps
- Nakhleh, Lowrey & Mitchell (1996) on narrowing the gap surfaced only as a citation. I found no controlled study showing the conceptual gap closed specifically in thermochemistry calculations.
- No meta-analysis of the conceptual-algorithmic gap was found.
- The source of the reasoning-skill conflict (Cracolice vs the Tsaparlis summary) could not be resolved from abstracts.

## Chemistry-specific tests of learning strategies: retrieval, spacing, interleaving, worked and erroneous examples, online homework, simulations

### Takeaway
Chemistry evidence broadly agrees in direction with the general learning literature but is thinner, mostly quasi-experimental and often lacks reported effect sizes. Best supported for this learner: mixed (uncategorised) question sets after first learning, testing with feedback, and worked examples for efficiency. Spacing in real chemistry courses is less certain than the lab evidence suggests, and erroneous examples remain unproven.

### Cited Findings
**Retrieval practice**
- Pyburn et al. (2014), general chemistry, within-subjects over two semesters, hierarchical linear models. Repeated testing before exams partially reduced the gap between low- and high-skilled comprehenders. The benefit came entirely from multiple-choice questions; elaborative interrogation (why-type) questions had a significant negative impact [A] — [Pyburn et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1021/ed4009045).
- Todd et al. (2021), n = 146, Introduction to Chemistry; post-exam retrieval quizzes. Those completing more than 50% scored over half a letter grade higher on the cumulative final, with no difference on during-term exams. Participation was self-selected, so this is not causal, although the during-term comparison reduces that concern [A] — [Todd et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1039/d0rp00185f).
- Vojdanoska et al. (2009/10): tested material was retained better than untested material, and feedback corrected errors. Group testing raised initial but not final test scores. Tertiary classroom; the abstract does not say chemistry, so it should not be counted as chemistry evidence [A] — [Vojdanoska et al.](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1630).

**Spacing**
- Bego et al. (2024), nine introductory STEM courses, quizzes massed or spaced. Significant effects only in Calculus I for Engineers and Chemistry for Health Professionals; small positive effects in General Chemistry and Diversity of Life. The meta-analysis was significant with all courses but not when calculus was excluded. The authors call generalisability unclear and suggest design features, such as multiple choice vs short answer and whether feedback is read, may matter [A] — [Bego et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40594-024-00468-5).
- Hopkins et al. (2016) is mathematics, in an engineering setting, not chemistry — [record](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-015-9349-8). Eglington & Pavlik (2020) is not chemistry either: simulation plus experiment on scheduling, with up to 40% more items recalled under model-optimised scheduling [A] — [npj Science of Learning 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-020-00074-4).

**Interleaving**
- Eglington & Kang (2017), undergraduates learning organic-compound categories from diagrams: interleaved beat blocked on novel examples two days later across simple and complex categories, even when diagnostic features were highlighted (4 experiments). This is category learning, not multi-step calculation [A] — [Eglington & Kang 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jarmac.2017.07.005).
- Gulacar et al. (2022): identical questions and practice length; mixed vs chapter-organised sets, three two-hour sessions. The mixed group gained more from the first session and the gap widened. n and effect size not in the abstract, and post-tests followed each session, so this says little about long delay [A] — [Gulacar et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1039/d1rp00334h).
- Cox & Gulacar (2024/25), two first-semester general chemistry cohorts with the same teaching: uncategorised homework scored higher on all four tests and the ACS final, significantly only on Test 1 and the final. Non-random cohorts [A] — [Cox & Gulacar](https://doi.org/10.1515/cti-2024-0083).
- A high-school action-research project on mole-mass conversions: interleaved group +13% on assessment, but blocked group +8% on the assignment, and the interleaved version took longer and felt harder. A low-rigour source, but a realistic picture of the trade-off [S] — [Master's project](https://nwcommons.nwciowa.edu/education_masters/285).

**Worked and erroneous examples (chemistry)**
- McLaren, Lim & Koedinger (2008), Stoichiometry Tutor, three studies: worked examples cut time by about 21%, with more learning from worked examples on conceptual items and from tutored solving on procedural items [S] — [eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/31r4s7c7) (the PDF text was unreadable through my tools, so check the excerpt against it).
- McLaren et al. (2016) compared worked examples, erroneous examples, tutored problems and problems to solve — [Computers in Human Behavior](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2015.08.038). I did not get its results. A search excerpt of the related 2014 conference chapter says worked examples were most efficient in time and mental effort, and suggests that worked examples and tutoring may suit lower prior knowledge while erroneous examples and plain problems may suit higher prior knowledge [S] — [chapter DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-07221-0_44). A later review lists McLaren et al. (2016) among studies showing no learning benefit from erroneous examples [S] — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/206221/1/5921-Article-45599-2-10-20210120.pdf).
- Crippen & Earl (2007), web-based, quasi-experimental: a summary apparently of this work says worked example plus self-explanation prompt improved performance, problem-solving skill and self-efficacy, and that worked examples alone were inconclusive [S] — [Crippen & Earl 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2005.11.018). Crippen & Brooks (2009) is a theoretical case for worked examples in chemistry, not new data [A] — [Crippen & Brooks 2009](https://doi.org/10.1039/b901458f).

**Online and adaptive homework**
- Eichler & Peeples (2013), regression controlling for background: precourse ALEKS completers scored about 13 points higher on a common final than non-participants, MasteringChemistry about 8, with more gain from all-quarter use. Participation was opt-in or opt-out, so selection is a real threat [A] — [Eichler & Peeples 2013](https://doi.org/10.1021/ed3006264). A later review notes the opt-out design [S] — [CERP 2021](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2021/rp/d0rp00283f).
- Richards-Babb et al. (2018), quasi-experimental with propensity matching, n = 6,114 pairs: adaptive homework increased the odds of a higher grade for average, below-average and failing students. Attitudes were slightly lower (3.15 vs 3.31 of 5), yet 95% reported remediating wrong answers and 69% reported changed study habits [A] — [Richards-Babb et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jchemed.7b00829). This is the strongest chemistry field evidence found, though it favours students below average and may say less about a motivated strong student. McGraw Hill ALEKS outcome pages are vendor claims and are excluded.

**PhET and simulations**
- Moore et al. (2013), 80 general chemistry students, ten minutes on Molecule Polarity with no instructions: groups explored on average 18 of 23 features, and 64% of utterances were part of on-topic discussion. This shows engagement, not learning gains [A] — [Moore et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1039/c3rp20157k).
- Salame & Makki (2021) is a perception survey, not a learning comparison [A] — [IJESE 2021](https://doi.org/10.21601/ijese/10966).
- Effect sizes from 0.7 to 1.48 come from small physics syntheses with weak reporting, and one newer analysis calls the evidence mixed. They should not be transferred to chemistry [S] — [physics meta-analysis example](https://ppjp.ulm.ac.id/journals/index.php/jipf/article/view/9046).
- Zhang & Linn (2011), middle-school students on hydrogen combustion: those who generated drawings integrated more ideas and interpreted the visualisation more precisely than those who ran more experiments with it [A] — [Zhang & Linn 2011](https://doi.org/10.1002/tea.20443).

### Inferences
- Likely useful default: first see a type with a worked example and self-explanation; then practise; then move fast to mixed sets with category labels hidden, since identifying the problem type is itself part of the skill and is what the exam demands. Gulacar's mismatch argument supports this directly.
- The Pyburn finding is a caution for card design. "Why" tiles are not shown to be a substitute for retrieval questions; explanation seems to help when it is tied to eliciting and repairing specific ideas (Teichert & Stacy, Crippen & Earl). Do the attempt first, then explain. The conflict is unresolved, and it is one study with one specific format.
- With limited time, simulations are low priority for energetics: no chemistry learning-gain evidence was found, and drawing did better than manipulating in the one direct comparison (in younger students).
- Erroneous examples should be treated as a low-cost, unproven supplement after basic competence, not as a core method. IB distractors give a ready-made supply (next sections).

### Gaps
- Pelley: nothing found on chemistry spacing or interleaving under that name. "Richards" in the brief appears to be Richards-Babb (online homework), not an interleaving author; "Hopkins" and "Eglington & Pavlik" are not chemistry studies. Karpicke surfaced only as non-chemistry science learning (via [Purdue release](https://www.purdue.edu/newsroom/research/2011/110120KarpickeScience.html)), and I found no Karpicke general-chemistry course study. The brief's attributions should not be repeated as chemistry evidence.
- From memory, McLaren et al. (2016) reported large time savings (roughly 46–68%) for worked examples in high-school stoichiometry with no learning loss. I could **not** verify this; do not cite it without checking the paper.
- A proxy mirror of the paywalled 2014 chapter was blocked by a permission control and I did not try to get around it. The full text of McLaren 2016 and Crippen & Earl remains unread.
- Rau (2016) on when multiple visual representations help has no retrievable abstract — [record](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-016-9365-3).
- No chemistry study was found with long-delay interleaving effects, or that tested these strategies in self-study by a single strong student. Nearly all populations are US or other university general chemistry, not IB.

## Topic-specific difficulties: energetics and enthalpy, atomic structure, misconceptions, refutation and conceptual change

### Takeaway
The bond-breaking and energy misconceptions are well documented across countries and levels, and IB reports show closely related errors, with the sign error having the most striking numbers. Evidence for Hess cycle and Born-Haber remedies specifically is thin to absent. Refutation-text benefit is well established generally (g ≈ 0.41), but chemistry-specific tests are few.

### Cited Findings
**Research on energetics**
- Galley (2004), start of an introductory physical chemistry course: the vast majority believed bond breaking is exothermic; over 80% picked bond breaking in reactants as the origin of a combustion's exothermicity. Drawing attention to it early "markedly" reduced later exam errors (uncontrolled instructor report) [A] — [Galley 2004](https://doi.org/10.1021/ed081p523).
- Boo (1998), 48 Grade 12 students interviewed after instruction, five familiar reactions: a range of conceptions at variance with the chemist's view, including some new to the literature [A] — [Boo 1998](https://doi.org/10.1002/(sici)1098-2736(199805)35:5%3C569::aid-tea6%3E3.0.co;2-n).
- Greenbowe & Meltzer (2003), 207 university students' exams plus one longitudinal interview subject: most calorimetry difficulties traced to not understanding that net changes in bond energy during aqueous reactions cause energy to move out of or into the total mass of the resulting solution [A] — [Greenbowe & Meltzer 2003](https://doi.org/10.1080/09500690305032).
- Becker & Cooper (2014), qualitative: undergraduates rely on intuition, on reading potential energy as "stored" energy and on heuristics, not on electrostatic reasoning [A] — [Becker & Cooper 2014](https://doi.org/10.1002/tea.21159). Cooper & Klymkowsky (2013) trace this to biology talk, macroscopic physics framing and chemistry's failure to link molecular with macroscopic energy (a perspective) [A] — [Cooper & Klymkowsky 2013](https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.12-10-0170).
- Nilsson & Niedderer (2013), small-scale Swedish studies: enthalpy treated as a form of energy, enthalpy and enthalpy change swapped, constant pressure stated and then ignored, work seen as only mechanical [A] — [Nilsson & Niedderer 2013](https://doi.org/10.1039/c2rp20135f).
- Sreenivasulu & Subramaniam (2013), Singapore chemistry majors, 106 in the main study, 30-item 4-tier test: 34 alternative conceptions; mean confidence was not very high even on correct answers [A] — [Sreenivasulu & Subramaniam](https://doi.org/10.1080/09500693.2012.683460).
- Turányi & Tóth (2013), Hungary: causes include everyday analogies, assigning macroscopic properties to particles and mixing thermodynamics with kinetics [A] — [Turányi & Tóth](https://doi.org/10.1039/c2rp20015e).
- UK undergraduates arrived with limited understanding of enthalpy change, with most conceptions unchanged after a thermodynamics lecture course; a common idea is that reactions occur because products have lower enthalpy, which makes endothermic reactions hard to explain [S] — [RSC Education download, attribution likely Carson & Watson but unconfirmed](https://edu.rsc.org/download?ac=517085).
- Indonesian Grade XI (n = 135): difficulty was concentrated where conceptual reasoning and maths combine (Hess's law, calorimetry, bond energy), with inconsistent answers on parallel test versions [S] — [JJEC](https://ejurnal.ung.ac.id/index.php/jjec/article/view/39096). A different Indonesian study reports rates of 90, 78, 45, 66 and 18% across exo/endothermic, thermochemical equations, enthalpy change, Hess's law and bond energy. These percentages are context-bound, from regional journals [S] — [JIPK](https://journal.unnes.ac.id/nju/JIPK/article/view/21629).
- Bain et al. (2014) is the standard review, but for upper-level university thermodynamics, not 16–19 [S] — [Bain et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1039/C4RP00011K).

**IB examiner evidence on R1.1 and R1.2 [L]** — all from [subject reports](file:///C:/Users/Arturo/Documents/ib_tee/data/chunks/subject_reports_2001_2025.jsonl), with `data/evidence/R1.1.json` and `R1.2.json`; refs are question codes, wording paraphrased.
- Sign errors, with distractor data: 24M.1.SL.TZ2.15, 39% correct and 37% chose the option with the wrong sign. 25M.1A.SL.TZ2.16, 61% correct and a quarter correctly computed but missed the negative sign. 25M.1A.HL.TZ2.19, 66% correct and 23% had the right value but no negative for combustion. 18N.2.SL.TZ0.7 (40%): mistakes mainly in signs of combustion enthalpies. 24N.1.HL.TZ0.18 (50%): forgot positive for endothermic.
- Linking exo/endothermic with temperature and stability: 25M.1A.SL.TZ2.15, 70% correct, with 13% and 11% getting one of the two features wrong. 25M.1A.HL.TZ3.20, just over 50% correct, with many saying temperature falls in an exothermic change.
- Q = mcΔT and calorimetry: 25M.2.HL.TZ3.9, about half made mistakes, using ΔH as Q, using a wrong mass (the 4.00 g given) as m, or missing the unit conversion (mean 0.9/2; 11% blank). 11M.2.HL.TZ2.1 and 18N.2.HL.TZ0.1 similar on mass, and on not dividing by moles. 15M.1.HL.TZ2.14: 68% correct, nearly 20% forgot to divide by 2. 24M.2.SL.TZ1.2: suggesting a "more accurate" thermometer, naming heat loss without how to prevent it, percentage error weak. The May 2025 Paper 2 TZ2 overview names unit checking as the most common mistake in enthalpy, entropy and ΔG calculations.
- Bond enthalpy: 25M.2.SL.TZ2.3, mean 1.34/3, wrong bonds selected from the booklet, ECF only if framed as broken minus made. 14N.2.HL.TZ0.2: reversed signs for broken vs formed, wrong number or type of bonds. 24M.2.HL.TZ1.4 and 25M.2.HL.TZ2.3: treating CO as having a C=O bond value. Average bond enthalpy definition was reported as weak in many sessions (e.g. 2011, 2012, 2015), and 11N.2.HL.TZ0.3 says "gaseous" and "similar" were often omitted.
- Hess cycles: 15M.2.HL.TZ1.6, those who did not draw the cycle tended to get it wrong (an examiner observation, not a controlled comparison). 09M.2.SL.TZ1.2: an unfamiliar cycle layout was often not recognised as Hess's law. 11N.1.SL.TZ0.16 and 12M.1.SL.TZ1.16: algebraic x, y, z drew teacher complaints, yet about 80% and 78% were correct.
- Born-Haber and lattice: 25M.1A.HL.TZ3.22, nearly 85% could identify electron affinity on a cycle. 14M.2.HL.TZ2.8: few could sum terms, and halving the Cl bond enthalpy and using MgCl₂ stoichiometry were missed. 19M.1.HL.TZ2.16 (72%): wrong states of ions in the lattice equation. 24M.1.HL.TZ2.18 commented on weak recall of lattice and hydration ideas.
- Rough scale from `macro_by_subtopic`: exam-frequency counts of 76 (R1.1) and 101 (R1.2); mean 57.1% correct over the 22 R1.1 items with data and 69.9% over the 19 R1.2 items with data. Regex tallies across 121 and 184 error sentences show, for R1.1, heat-loss/practical 17, units/mass/moles 15 and sign 14; for R1.2, definition/gaseous/average 24, units/mass 19, sign 18, Hess 14 and broken/formed 13. Limits: regex categories overlap, tags have noise (e.g. a buffer finding tagged R1.1), and R1.2's listed error sentences stop at 2019, so 2025 evidence for it is sparse.
- Syllabus scope: complete construction of a Born-Haber cycle will not be assessed, but interpreting and determining values is, for univalent and divalent ions. Q = mcΔT, ΔHf and ΔHc equations are in the data booklet. Energy profile axes should be labelled reaction coordinate and potential energy (`data/evidence/R1.1.json`, `R1.2.json`, from the guide).

**Atomic structure**
- Tsaparlis & Papaphotis (2009), 12th grade age 17–18, n = 125 plus 23 interviewed: planetary Bohr model strongly favoured, orbital probability absent for many, hybrid models common; the conceptual-change approach worked in some cases but not others [A] — [Tsaparlis & Papaphotis](https://doi.org/10.1080/09500690801891908).
- Ionisation energy: Singapore A-level students (16–19) used the same octet-rule and "conservation of force" frameworks as UK students [A] — [Tan et al. 2005, OpenAlex search; the record has no DOI](https://api.openalex.org/works?search=two-tier%20diagnostic%20instrument%20A-level%20ionisation%20energy). Turkish grades 9–12, n = 956: same two frameworks [A] — [Nakıboğlu & Cosgun 2017](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/pub/epess/issue/30769/335780).

**Refutation and conceptual change**
- Schroeder & Kucera (2022), 44 comparisons, n = 3,869: refutation text g = 0.41, robust across contexts [S] — [Educational Psychology Review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-021-09656-z). Danielson et al. (2024), 76 studies, 294 effect sizes: consistent advantage, no moderators changed it [S] — [PAR record](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10629626). A biology-focused meta-analysis flags publication bias and sample-size influence [S] — [Turku record](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/181440214).
- Chemistry-specific: Beerenwinkel et al. (2011), 214 students, particle model, conceptual-change text beat traditional text on awareness of misconceptions [A] — [Beerenwinkel et al.](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10763-010-9257-9). Teichert & Stacy and Galley (above) are the bond-energy analogues.

### Inferences
- A short list of refutation notes looks justified for R1.1 and R1.2, each stating the wrong idea, why it attracts, and the correct model: bond breaking absorbs energy; temperature rise means an exothermic reaction with lower-enthalpy products; Q from the mass of solution or water and not solute; ΔH sign set by the process and not the arithmetic; broken minus formed for bond enthalpies vs products minus reactants for ΔHf; average bond enthalpy requires gaseous species and similar compounds.
- A compulsory final "sign and unit check" step on energetics calculations addresses the most numerically documented lost marks, at nearly no time cost. Distractor data suggest this error sits with people who did the arithmetic correctly.
- The ΔHf and bond-enthalpy formula direction reverses, which is a good target for mixed practice, because blocked practice would hide the choice between them. Inference only.

### Gaps
- No rigorous study was found for teaching Hess cycles or Born-Haber at 16–19. What surfaced is a practitioner idea ([RSC Education](https://edu.rsc.org/ideas/bring-hesss-cycles-to-life/4021571.article)), a 15-student Malaysian qualitative study ([ABIIS](https://doi.org/10.24191/bav4i4/80756)) and regional quasi-experiments of low reporting quality.
- Barker & Millar's A-level work and Hadfield & Wieman's on first-law equations did not resolve in my searches.
- Local tallies are descriptive and do not show how many candidates made each error.

## Representations: diagrams, energy cycles, how students read them, drawing and simulations

### Takeaway
Experts move between representations and novices stay within surface features: this is well established descriptively. Student drawing often reveals misunderstanding that writing hides. That drawing improves learning in chemistry is promising, but it rests on few experiments, and none found on energy cycles.

### Cited Findings
- Kozma & Russell (1997), chemists vs undergraduates: novices formed smaller, same-media groups on surface features; experts formed larger, cross-media groups for conceptual reasons, and were better at transforming representations, especially giving verbal descriptions. They argue for a larger role for multiple representations, particularly linguistic ones [A] — [Kozma & Russell 1997](https://doi.org/10.1002/(sici)1098-2736(199711)34:9%3C949::aid-tea7%3E3.0.co;2-u).
- Popova & Bretz (2018), 36 organic chemistry II students: difficulty explaining peaks, valleys, height and width. They conflated transition states and intermediates despite reciting definitions, and described species along the x-axis while largely ignoring y-axis energies [A] — [Popova & Bretz 2018](https://doi.org/10.1039/c8rp00063h). A later general chemistry study (16 students) reports reading the x-axis as time and sometimes the y-axis as concentration [S] — [CERP 2021](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2021/rp/d1rp00037c).
- Cooper, Williams & Underwood (2015): at least 55% drew each intermolecular force as within a molecule, only 10–30% as between molecules, and 59% were inconsistent. Written descriptions were often too ambiguous to tell [A] — [Cooper et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jchemed.5b00169).
- Cooper, Stieff & DeSutter (2017) argue sketching supports model-based reasoning and shows instructors students' mental models (a conceptual paper) [A] — [Cooper et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/tops.12285). Ainsworth et al. (2011) make the general case that drawing should be an explicit part of science education (a short perspective) [A] — [Ainsworth et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1204153).
- Zhang & Linn (2011) (above) is the closest controlled comparison: generating drawings beat extra simulation interaction, in middle-school students [A] — [Zhang & Linn 2011](https://doi.org/10.1002/tea.20443).
- Eglington & Kang (2017) (above) suggests interleaving diagrams helps classify new chemical structures [A] — [Eglington & Kang 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jarmac.2017.07.005).
- IB signals [L]: 15M.2.HL.TZ1.6 linked not drawing the cycle with wrong answers; 09M.2.SL.TZ1.2 showed unfamiliar layouts were not recognised; 12M.1.HL.TZ1.16 drew teacher complaints about a non-standard Born-Haber layout, even though 67% got it right; May 2025 Paper 2 TZ2 advised practice sketching and labelling graphs; May 2025 energy profile items were generally done well (25M.2.HL.TZ2.3; also listed as well prepared in R1.1 findings) — [subject reports](file:///C:/Users/Arturo/Documents/ib_tee/data/chunks/subject_reports_2001_2025.jsonl).

### Inferences
- For Hess and Born-Haber questions, require sketching the cycle before calculation; keep arrows and signs on the sketch. Support is indirect (one examiner remark plus overload logic).
- Practice should vary layouts (cycle triangle, vertical Born-Haber ladder, algebra-only) so the student recognises the structure regardless of form. Examiner comments specifically report recognition failure with unfamiliar forms.
- A good translation drill is profile diagram to sentence to equation to number, since expert skill appears as translation, particularly into words. For energetics this has been inferred, not tested.
- Revision notes should show the diagram alongside the verbal rule and not as standalone pictures, and should prompt redrawing from memory. Reading a diagram is not evidence of understanding it (Popova & Bretz).

### Gaps
- No study was found of how 16–19 students read Hess or Born-Haber cycles specifically. Reaction coordinate work is on organic and general chemistry undergraduates.
- I found no chemistry RCT of drawing vs not drawing on exam performance among upper-secondary students.

## IB Chemistry assessment: 2025 structure, weightings, command terms, recurring errors, past papers and markschemes

### Takeaway
Marks and timings are verified on actual May and November 2025 cover pages. Paper 1B has been a notable hurdle (mean about 43% in one time zone), driven by practical understanding, so purely theoretical energetics revision will miss marks. Evidence that doing past papers works is indirect but favourable if the student attempts first and self-marks, with real concern about narrowing to markscheme recall, which IB examiners themselves warn about.

### Cited Findings
**Structure (verified where marked)**

**How the papers behave (2025 reports) [L]** — [subject reports](file:///C:/Users/Arturo/Documents/ib_tee/data/chunks/subject_reports_2001_2025.jsonl)
- Paper 1A: guidance is about a minute per question; work maths as short-answer; read every option; cross out; some distractors are true statements that do not answer the question; working out what mistake each distractor represents is described as good practice (TZ1, TZ2). **Past-paper practice and markschemes**
- Balch (1998), random assignment, introductory psychology: taking a sample exam and self-scoring beat just seeing the key, on a final a week later; effect did not interact with class standing. Not chemistry, multiple-choice only [A] — [Balch 1998](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15328023top2503_3).

### Inferences
- If 1A and 1B marks count equally within Paper 1 (my assumption, not checked): 1 Paper 1 mark ≈ 0.48% of the final grade and 1 Paper 2 mark ≈ 0.49%, so 1A ≈ 19%, 1B ≈ 17% and Paper 2 = 44%. Marks are about equal in value across papers.
- Timing: at the suggested minute per 1A question, about 40 minutes for 1A leaves about 80 minutes for 35 marks of 1B (roughly 2.3 minutes per mark, vs about 1.67 on Paper 2). Worth testing under timed conditions; this is arithmetic, not official guidance.
- Format mapping for R1.1 and R1.2:
  - 1A: fast discrimination among near-identical options; sign and stability/temperature pairing; recall definitions. Practise without leaning on the booklet.
  - 1B: calorimetry as an experiment: sources of error, how to reduce heat loss, precision vs accuracy, graph extrapolation, uncertainties. Examiner comments suggest this is where theory-only revision is most exposed.
  - Paper 2: multi-part calculation with ECF, showing working, definitions (average bond enthalpy, ΔHf), explanation tied to command term and marks.
- Sensible past-paper routine: attempt first, closed-book; self-mark line by line; log the error type; then redo a variant later. Self-scoring beats reading the key on the limited evidence (Balch, Sanchez).
- Use markschemes to learn what counts as an adequate answer and how ECF works. But do not memorise phrasing as the goal: the Torrance risk is exactly what the 2025 reports warn about, and the 2025 syllabus is new enough that old-paper wording may mislead. Include some novel, reformatted questions alongside past papers.
- Pre-2025 questions remain useful for calculation and concept practice but do not sample 1B's style. The first genuine 1B data are few (May and Nov 2025), so these should be saved for timed use and not burnt on early learning.
