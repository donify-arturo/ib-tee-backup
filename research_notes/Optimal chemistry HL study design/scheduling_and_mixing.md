# Scheduling and mixing practice across sessions for IB Chemistry HL (past-paper self-study)

Evidence labels used throughout:
- **[DIRECT]**: school or university STEM problem solving, classroom setting.
- **[NEAR]**: school or university classroom, but not chemistry problem solving (for example psychology concepts, mathematics).
- **[EXTRAPOLATED]**: lab or fact/word-pair learning, being applied here to chemistry problem solving.

No randomised trial was found of any full scheduling system for 16-19-year-olds doing chemistry past papers. Everything below on that exact case is inference from nearby evidence.

## Retrieval practice (testing effect): how large, and does it hold for complex problem solving?

### Takeaway
Retrieval with feedback reliably beats restudy for facts and concepts, with typical g about 0.3-0.7 and a classroom overall g of about 0.50. The evidence for multi-step problem solving is weaker and mixed: it looks positive when learners get feedback and vary the problems, and about zero in the worked-example lab studies, so past-paper practice should be treated as "attempt, then check against the markscheme", not as pure recall.

### Cited Findings
**Meta-analyses**
- Rowland 2014, testing vs restudy, 159 effect sizes from 61 studies: g = 0.50, 95% CI [0.42, 0.58]. Mostly college samples. [EXTRAPOLATED: Yang et al. report that 81% of Rowland's effects used word pairs or word lists] — [Rowland 2014 full text](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf); [Yang 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)
- Rowland moderators:
  - Retention interval under 1 day g = 0.41 vs 1 day or more g = 0.69.
  - Feedback g = 0.73 vs no feedback g = 0.39.
  - Without feedback, the effect tracks initial success: 50% or less correct g = 0.03, 51-75% g = 0.29, over 75% g = 0.56.
  - Cued recall initial test g = 0.61 vs recognition g = 0.29.
  - Prose materials g = 0.58 (k = 23).
  - Within-subject designs g = 0.43 vs between-subject g = 0.69 — [Rowland 2014](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf)
- Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan 2017, 118 articles, 272 effect sizes, 15,472 participants. Practice tests beat restudy and all other comparison conditions. The headline is reported second-hand as g = 0.61 (fixed) and 0.70 (random). I could not read the full text, so check these before quoting — [SAGE record](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654316689306); [secondary summary](https://studycardsai.com/blog/active-recall-statistics)
- Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu & Shanks 2021, classroom only, 48,478 students, 222 studies, 573 effects: overall g = 0.499. Authors judge publication-bias risk small from p-curve analysis. A bias-corrected g of 0.427 appears only in secondary summaries — [Yang 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf); [secondary](https://tipsforteachers.substack.com/p/research-bite-4-testing-quizzing)
- Yang moderators most relevant here [NEAR/DIRECT]:
  - High school g = 0.655 [0.496, 0.815], k = 49.
  - Chemistry g = 0.340 [0.027, 0.654], k = 10. Subject did not significantly moderate overall (Q = 13.64, p = .69), so do not read chemistry as truly lower.
  - Comparison with restudy g = 0.330 [0.256, 0.404]. The 0.50 headline includes weaker controls.
  - Exam content: fact g = 0.524, concept g = 0.644, problem solving g = 0.453 [0.309, 0.596]. Content type was not a significant moderator (p = .087).
  - Feedback g = 0.537 vs none g = 0.374.
  - Repetitions: 1 test g = 0.444, 2 g = 0.601, 3 g = 0.642, unlimited g = 0.762.
  - Identical questions g = 0.512, rephrased g = 0.558, untested material g = 0.321.
  - Post-class g = 0.536 vs pre-class g = 0.186.
  - Multiple choice g = 0.567 — [Yang 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)
- Caveat on Yang's "problem solving" code: its example is applying clinical techniques to new scenarios, so it is not specifically quantitative STEM problem solving. Classroom quizzing also bundles extra exposure and motivation, which the authors treat as part of the mechanism — [Yang 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)
- Agarwal, Nunes & Blunt 2021, narrow-definition classroom review: 50 experiments, 49 effect sizes, n = 5,374, 57% medium or large. One summary reports 40% secondary, 40% undergraduate and 20% medical settings, and the authors note little dependence on exact format or timing. These details come from summaries, not the full text — [record](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1371679026699517952); [database](https://retrievalpractice.org/review); [TES summary](https://www.tes.com/magazine/teaching-learning/general/why-retrieval-practice-safe-bet-teachers)
- Pan & Rickard 2018, transfer of testing, 192 effects, 122 experiments, N = 10,382: d = 0.40 [0.31, 0.50]. Application and inference questions d = 0.32. After publication-bias correction, intercepts often predict no positive transfer unless elaborated retrieval, response congruency or high initial test performance are present — [Pan & Rickard 2018](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PR_2018.pdf)
- Dunlosky et al. 2013 rate practice testing and distributed practice "high utility", interleaving "moderate" (evidence then limited), and rereading and highlighting "low" — [Dunlosky 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)

**Complex materials and problem solving: the counter-evidence**
- Pan & Rickard, problem-solving category: d = 0.29 [0.078, 0.65], p = .10, 17-18 effects. It splits sharply: worked-example studies about zero (|d| = 0.045; the sign is unclear in my text extraction) vs medical diagnosis and treatment about 0.59. The authors stress the two subcategories are confounded: worked-example studies were lab-based and generally gave no feedback — [Pan & Rickard 2018](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PR_2018.pdf)
- van Gog & Sweller 2015: the testing effect shrinks or disappears as element interactivity rises. Their special issue includes an empirical paper finding that testing after worked-example study did not beat restudy for delayed problem solving — [van Gog & Sweller 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-015-9310-x)
- Karpicke & Aue 2015 reply: element interactivity was never measurably defined, studies showing effects with complex materials were omitted, the null results reflect the retrieval tasks used, and reanalysis shows a small positive effect with worked examples. Rawson 2015 ("still a winner") also calls for more empirical work on complex materials. The debate is unresolved — [Karpicke & Aue 2015](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-015-9309-3); [Rawson 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-015-9308-4)
- Yeo & Fazio (2018/2019): for a novel mathematics procedure, the better strategy (retrieval vs worked examples) depended on retention interval and on whether practice problems were identical or varied. Rohrer et al. summarise this as mixed evidence for retrieval practice with mathematics. I saw summaries only — [guest post by the authors](https://learningscientists.org/blog/2018/4/11-1); [Rohrer 2019](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2019-rohrer.pdf)
- Positive science-learning example [NEAR]: Karpicke & Blunt 2011 found retrieval beat concept mapping on inference and comprehension questions with science texts — [Karpicke & Blunt 2011](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199327)
- Format conflict: Adesope is reported to find multiple choice stronger than short answer, while a pure-testing university lecture study (N = 92) found a benefit for short answer and none for multiple choice, and only for retrievable content — [Greving & Richter 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/pdf)

### Inferences
- A realistic planning figure for retrieval vs restudy in a motivated student is g about 0.3-0.5. Do not promise more. Yang's 0.65 for high school includes weaker control conditions.
- App rule: every question attempt should end with immediate markscheme comparison. Feedback nearly doubles Rowland's effect (0.39 to 0.73), and without feedback low-success attempts yield about nothing (g = 0.03). Multi-step tasks are exactly where success rates start low.
- For new or low-success topics, use a worked example or a hint-supported attempt first, because the null results cluster around unsupported retrieval of multi-step procedures. Once a student succeeds about 75% of the time, retrieval without hints is the productive mode.
- Use varied or rephrased questions rather than repeats. Rephrased items did no worse than identical ones in Yang, and exam transfer is what matters.

### Gaps
- No meta-analytic estimate exists specifically for 16-19-year-olds solving chemistry calculation or mechanism problems. Chemistry k = 10 in Yang is all levels and mixed outcomes.
- The Adesope full text, including its secondary-school subgroup, could not be read.
- Whether elaborated retrieval moderators apply to quantitative chemistry is unknown.

## Spaced practice: optimal gap, expanding vs equal intervals, and classroom STEM evidence

### Takeaway
Spacing is among the most robust effects. The best gap grows with time-to-exam but shrinks as a share of it (about 20-40% at one week, about 5-10% at one year), and the data are for fact recall. Equal vs expanding intervals appears not to matter, so the app should maximise absolute spacing and not fuss over the pattern.

### Cited Findings
- Cepeda et al. 2006: 839 assessments, 317 experiments, 184 articles. Optimal gap rises with retention interval. Only 4% of the performance differences in its lag analyses used retention intervals over 1 month, and 80% used under 1 day [EXTRAPOLATED] — [Cepeda 2006](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaPashlerVulWixtedRohrer-PB-2006.pdf)
- Cepeda et al. 2008: over 1,350 online learners studied facts, reviewed after a gap of up to 3.5 months, and were tested up to 1 year later. As the gap grows, performance rises and then gradually falls. Optimal gap falls from about 20-40% of a 1-week delay to about 5-10% of a 1-year delay [EXTRAPOLATED] — [Cepeda 2008 abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)
- Cepeda 2008 interpolated optimal gaps for recall (single review):

| Test delay | Interpolated optimal gap | Share of delay |
|---|---|---|
| 7 days | about 3 days | 43% |
| 35 days | about 8 days | 23% |
| 70 days | about 12 days | 17% |
| 350 days | about 27 days | 8% |

  Optimal vs zero gap gave a 64% increase in recall (d = 1.1) and a 26% increase in recognition (d = 1.5), with study time equated — [Cepeda 2008 full text](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)
- Cepeda 2008 caution: compressing a topic into a too-short window gives misleadingly high immediate mastery. Their advice to decide first how long you need to remember is a direct design input — [Cepeda 2008](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)
- Latimier, Peyre & Ramus 2021, 29 studies: spaced vs massed retrieval g = 0.74; expanding vs uniform schedules g = 0.034 (ns). Abstract excerpts only; moderators not read — [Latimier 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8); [preprint record](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.31234/osf.io/kzy7u)
- Karpicke & Bauernschmidt 2011: long intervals between 3 tests gave a 200% long-term retention gain over no spacing, but expanding, equal and contracting schedules did not differ. Lab, recall items [EXTRAPOLATED] — [Karpicke & Bauernschmidt 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023436)
- Older view: Cepeda 2006 found expanding numerically better overall, but called this tentative given large standard errors and conflicting studies, and 15 of the 18 studies were paired associates — [Cepeda 2006](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaPashlerVulWixtedRohrer-PB-2006.pdf)

**Classroom STEM and mathematics**
- Emeny, Hartwig & Rohrer 2021, two classroom experiments, Year 7 UK mathematics (n = 44, 55): practice split across 3 sessions spaced 1 week apart vs one session, test 1 month later. Spacing d = 0.61 [0.28, 0.92] and d = 0.39 [0.11, 0.66] (conservative d_z). Students aged 11-12, so younger than this learner [NEAR] — [Emeny 2021](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2021-emeny.pdf)
- Hopkins, Lyle, Hieb & Ralston 2016, engineering pre-calculus: students with some spaced quiz items did better on the course final and on later calculus exams than fully massed students. Effect sizes not retrieved; secondary summary. Close analogue for STEM [DIRECT, but mathematics] — [authors' guest post](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2016/10/11-1)
- Lyle et al. 2020, pre-calculus: retention rose when quiz items were spaced, not when the number of quiz questions increased. The authors advise increasing spacing rather than amount. Conference summary read; full paper not read — [ASEE paper](https://peer.asee.org/29900)
- Limits and nulls: Ebersbach & Barzagar Nazari 2020 (lab) were non-significant with a 5-week test delay, though scores rose with gap (about 25% massed, about 30% 1-day gap, about 40% 11-day gap). Emeny et al. suggest short gaps and short test delays may shrink or remove spacing effects in mathematics — [Emeny 2021](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2021-emeny.pdf)
- Time-of-study caveat from Rawson & Dunlosky 2013 (567 students, key-term definitions): benefits of spacing during initial learning are strongly reduced once later relearning sessions occur. So what matters most is having later sessions — [Rawson & Dunlosky 2013](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030498)

### Inferences
- Rule of thumb for the app: next review gap about 25% of days-to-exam at 2-5 weeks out, about 15% at 2-3 months, about 10% at 5-6 months. These are my interpolations from fact-recall data with a single review, not measured chemistry optima. The curve falls only gradually past the peak, so err slightly long rather than short.
- Use simple equal or gently growing gaps. No need to build elaborate expanding ladders.
- Spacing and retrieval combine: a spaced session should be a spaced attempt, not a spaced reread.

### Gaps
- No study has estimated optimal gap for multi-step chemistry problem solving. Skills might decay more slowly than arbitrary facts, or more quickly if procedural steps are unpractised; unknown.
- I did not retrieve numeric results from Carpenter's reviews (including Carpenter, Pan & Butler 2022), and no study was found that fixes a percentage rule for multi-session schedules.

## Interleaving: where it helps, where it fails, and when blocking should come first

### Takeaway
For mathematics-like problem solving, mixing problem types improves delayed tests (RCT d = 0.83, though mathematics meta-analytic g = 0.34) by making the student choose the strategy. Blocked practice should come first for new skills, and the evidence is not clean for long multi-part tasks or chemistry specifically.

### Cited Findings
**The main trial**
- Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung 2020 (online 2019), preregistered cluster RCT: 54 Grade 7 mathematics classes, 15 teachers, 787 analysed students (8% attrition, about equal by group). Eight practice worksheets of 8 problems over about 103 days, a shared interleaved review about 10 days later, then an unannounced test about 33 days after that. Interleaved 61% vs blocked 38%, d = 0.83 [0.68, 0.97]. Positive for every teacher; pilot d = 0.97 [NEAR: ages about 12-13, mathematics] — [Rohrer 2019](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2019-rohrer.pdf); [CI summary](https://zendy.io/pdf-viewer/10.1037/edu0000367)
- The authors' own caveats [NEAR]:
  1. Interleaving probably takes longer (every teacher said so; time on task not measured), so the effect per minute would be smaller.
  2. It may be smaller at short test delays.
  3. It may be less effective or too hard without at least a small amount of blocked practice first (the study's students likely had that from teachers).
  4. Corrective feedback might be necessary. Students saw solutions and corrected errors, as in all previous studies.
  
  They also note the comparison is a high vs low dose of interleaving, and that the effect combines strategy choice, spacing and retrieval, so it is not a pure "mixing" effect — [Rohrer 2019](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2019-rohrer.pdf)
- Test-delay moderation, one study: d = 0.42 at 1 day vs d = 0.79 at 30 days (cited in Brunmair & Richter as Rohrer et al.). The only earlier null in this line (Ostrow et al. 2015) had a 2-5 day delay — [Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf); [Rohrer 2019](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2019-rohrer.pdf)

**Meta-analysis: where it helps and does not**
- Brunmair & Richter 2019, 59 studies, 238 effects, 158 samples: overall g = 0.42. Paintings g = 0.67; photographs g = 0.35; mathematical tasks g = 0.34 [0.11, 0.57]; expository texts g = 0.21 (ns); words g = -0.39 [-0.64, -0.14], favouring blocking. Very high heterogeneity (I² about 77%; sample effects from -1.37 to 1.85) — [Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)
- Moderators: bigger effects with items more similar between categories, less similar within categories, and more complex material. Larger in younger samples. Retention interval was not a moderator overall. In articles testing temporal spacing, interleaving worked for immediate succession (g = 0.73) but not when items were spaced apart (g = 0.22, ns; k = 17, inductive-learning studies) [EXTRAPOLATED] — [Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)
- Authors explicitly note the small mathematics average does not mean it is ineffective: implementation and outcome measures can make it help or harm — [Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)
- Firth, Rivers & Boyle 2021, concept-learning systematic review (26 studies; 17 in meta-analysis): up to g = 0.65 for memory, up to 0.66 for transfer, greatest where differences are subtle. **None of the studies used high-school participants**, mostly undergraduates. Benefits were restricted to visual examples or very short texts, and the authors caution against generalising to lengthy educational tasks. Little evidence for or against combining blocked and interleaved schedules — [Firth 2021](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/75711/7/Firth_etal_RE_2021_A_systematic_review_of_interleaving.pdf)

**Chemistry and physics**
- Samani & Pan 2021, UCLA physics for life-science majors, about 290 analysed, counterbalanced within-subject, preregistered for overall outcomes. Homework was 3 problems per topic (blocked) vs 1 per topic (interleaved), 84 problems per 4-week stage. Surprise tests with novel harder problems: overall d = 0.40 and 0.91; formula recall d = 0.41 and 0.96; correct solution d = 0.25 and 0.40. **Midterm differences were not significant** (d = 0.20, 0.02), perhaps because most students crammed. Interleaved homework accuracy was lower (0.69 vs 0.74, 0.67 vs 0.76). Limits: single course, the instructor was an author, sub-measures exploratory, long-term retention not established [DIRECT, undergraduate] — [Samani & Pan 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8589969/)
- Gulacar et al. 2022 (Chem. Educ. Res. Pract. 23, 422-435), 79 university general chemistry students, three problem-solving sessions, mixed vs topic-grouped: mixed group improved more in step-level success (successful step codes up about 52%, unsuccessful down about 40%). A-, B- and C-grade students benefited, B and C most. The summary notes numerical topics may need extensive blocked practice first. Small; allocation method not verified; I read the RSC summary only [DIRECT] — [RSC Education summary](https://edu.rsc.org/education-research/tweak-your-questions-to-improve-students-problem-solving-skills/4015382.article)
- Eglington & Kang 2017, organic structure classification: 2 days later interleaved 85% vs blocked 71%; with more complex materials 65% vs 49%. This is categorisation of structures, not synthesis or calculation; secondary summary [NEAR, chemistry content] — [Learning Scientists](https://www.learningscientists.org/learning-scientists-podcast/2017/12/20/episode-9-bite-size-research-on-interleaving-categories)
- One high-school mole-mass study (n = 25, master's thesis) reports better delayed retention. Very weak evidence, but the only school-age chemistry study found — [thesis](https://nwcommons.nwciowa.edu/education_masters/285)

**Mechanism**
- Strategy choice: with blocked sets the student already knows the method before reading the problem. Mixed sets force selecting the strategy from the problem itself, as on a cumulative exam — [Rohrer 2019](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2019-rohrer.pdf)
- Contrast between confusable categories is the theoretical account, supported by the similarity moderators — [Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)

### Inferences
- Sequence for a new or weak skill: worked example, then a few blocked attempts until one is done unaided, then mixed sets for the rest. Blocked is for acquiring the procedure, mixed is for choosing it. The size of the blocked dose is unknown, so keep it small except for numerical topics, per the Gulacar summary.
- Chemistry-specific mixing targets (my judgement, untested): confusable pairs where strategy choice is the real difficulty, such as buffer vs strong/weak acid pH, SN1 vs SN2, Kc vs Kp vs ICE setups, enthalpy cycle types, oxidation/reduction identification. Mixing unrelated topics probably gives spacing but less contrast.
- Because the immediate-succession result suggests contrast needs adjacency, mix confusable types within a session. Spreading them across days gives spacing but does not substitute for contrast.
- Interleave at question-part or short-question level by default. Long multi-part paper questions are where Firth warns against generalising, and a whole paper is itself natural mixing.
- Report only delayed performance as progress, since interleaved practice accuracy will be lower.
- Interleaving must not be shown as failure: Samani & Pan's lower homework scores are expected.

### Gaps
- No RCT of interleaving in 16-19-year-old chemistry was found. Evidence for Paper 2-style extended response is absent.
- The ideal blocked-to-mixed ratio and the optimal number of mixed categories are unknown.
- Samani & Pan's null on midterms means benefit on real exams under cramming is uncertain. The Rohrer effect is on an unannounced test without cramming.

## Scheduling algorithms and successive relearning: value for problem solving

### Takeaway
Modern algorithms predict flashcard recall better, but none has been validated for chemistry problem solving, and benefit to exam outcomes is mostly unmeasured. Successive relearning (3 correct recalls initially, then 3 spaced relearning sessions) has solid classroom support for definitions, so it's the best-supported structure to borrow, with intervals driven by time-to-exam rather than a fitted model.

### Cited Findings
**Algorithms: what is measured**
- Leitner (1972): box system, promoted on success, demoted on failure. Settles & Meeder 2016, 12.9M Duolingo instances: mean absolute error HLR 0.128, Leitner 0.235, Pimsleur 0.445, but Leitner AUC 0.542 vs HLR 0.538, so HLR's gain was calibration, not discrimination [EXTRAPOLATED: vocabulary] — [Settles & Meeder 2016](https://aclanthology.org/P16-1174.pdf)
- HLR vs Leitner A/B test at Duolingo: daily return for any activity +0.3%, lesson +0.3%, practice -7.3%. A later lexical-feature-free version vs HLR: +12.0%, +1.7%, +9.5%. The outcome was daily engagement, not learning; the authors are a company team — [Settles & Meeder 2016](https://aclanthology.org/P16-1174.pdf)
- FSRS benchmark, 9,999 Anki collections, about 350M evaluated reviews, log loss without same-day reviews (lower is better): FSRS-7 0.3401 (AUC 0.717), FSRS-6 0.3460, FSRS-5 0.3561, HLR 0.4694, Ebisu v2 0.4989. **A zero-parameter moving-average baseline scores 0.3369 (AUC 0.700)**, as good as plain FSRS-7 on log loss though worse on AUC. The benchmark measures prediction, not learning; data are flashcards only — [srs-benchmark](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark)
- FSRS-6 (with recency weighting) has lower log loss than Anki's SM-2 for 99.6% of users. SM-2 was not designed to output probabilities, so the comparison is not fully fair. The benchmark is maintained by the FSRS community, and SuperMemo disputes the metrics, but it has its own commercial interest — [Expertium benchmark page](https://expertium.github.io/Benchmark.html); [SuperMemo blog](https://supermemo.com/en/blog/supermemo-is-better-than-fsrs-by-far)
- FSRS desired retention defaults to 0.9. Workload is U-shaped vs retention, and optimum depends on learner and horizon. Forum reports suggest simulators lean toward low targets, which is an unreviewed community view — [FSRS wiki, read through a mirror](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/wiki/The-Optimal-Retention); [Jiten guide](https://jiten.moe/guides/tuning-fsrs); [forum thread](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/fixing-low-optimal-retention-bias/68506)

**Algorithms: outcome trials**
- Lindsey, Shroyer, Pashler & Mozer 2014, middle school Spanish: personalised review +16.5% over massed and +10% over generic spacing on a cumulative exam after the semester, about 30 minutes a week reported. The best classroom outcome evidence for model-driven review, but vocabulary [EXTRAPOLATED] — [Lindsey 2014](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797613504302); [press release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/579380)
- Mettler, Massey & Kellman 2016, undergraduates, African country locations (n = 72 + 48): adaptive schedule using accuracy and response time beat fixed schedules at 1-week delay (0.42 vs 0.31 and 0.30; d about 0.56), and expanding and equal fixed schedules did not differ (p = .65). Small; fact learning [EXTRAPOLATED] — [Mettler 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6028005)

**Successive relearning**
- Rawson & Dunlosky 2011, 533 students, conceptual material, more than 100,000 hand-scored responses, 1-4 correct recalls initially, 1-5 relearning sessions, tested at 1-4 months. Criterion effects were strong before relearning but diminished with it, and relearning gave large retention gains for few extra trials. Their prescriptive rule: **3 correct recalls initially, then relearn 3 times at widely spaced intervals** — [Rawson & Dunlosky 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023956)
- Rawson, Dunlosky & Sciartelli 2013, Introductory Psychology course: raised exam performance about a letter grade (C to B) vs baseline. Summary read; full paper not read — [summary](http://www.retrievalpractice.org/strategies/2018/successive-relearning); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-013-9240-4)
- Janes, Dunlosky, Rawson & Jasnow 2020, high-stakes exams in a hard biopsychology course: at least 10% improvement, d from 0.54 to 1.10 — [ERIC record](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/eric__EJ1265965)
- Higham et al. 2022, introductory psychology: fill-in-the-blank relearning with feedback beat restudy for end-of-semester recall — [record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_2861332799)
- Rawson & Dunlosky 2022 review (via secondary): one-week retention 68% for one correct recall in each of 3 spaced sessions vs 26% for 3 correct recalls in one session. The research base is limited and mostly vocabulary and definitions, and optimal scheduling likely varies with materials. Full text was blocked — [Firth summary](https://firth.substack.com/p/learning-and-re-learning); [SAGE record](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09637214221100484)
- Vaughn & Rawson 2011: higher criterion improves association, cue and target memory (Lithuanian-English pairs) [EXTRAPOLATED] — [Vaughn & Rawson 2011](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611417724)

### Inferences
- Do not fit FSRS or HLR per question for this app. Each past-paper question can only be seen a few times before the answer is memorised (Rohrer's RCT never repeated a problem), there is too little data from one student, and these models were trained on repeated identical flashcards. The moving-average baseline's strength hints that a simple rule may not lose much, though it is a predictor and was never tested as a scheduler.
- Schedule at the **skill level** (syllabus understanding or technique card), selecting a fresh isomorphic past-paper question on each review. Keep a separate small flashcard layer only for pure recall items such as definitions and data-booklet knowledge. FSRS or Leitner is defensible there.
- Practical default: a Leitner-style skill state with gaps capped by the time-to-exam rule from the spacing section. It is explainable, resumable and needs no training.
- Treat "3 + 3" as a starting heuristic for problem skills: 1-2 unaided correct attempts initially (one multi-step correct is much costlier than a flashcard) and at least 3 spaced correct sessions. Rawson's data suggest relearning sessions matter more than initial criterion, so cut initial reps first when short of time.

### Gaps
- I found **no** head-to-head trial of Leitner, SM-2, FSRS or HLR on problem-solving outcomes.
- FSRS parameter behaviour with a few hundred reviews from one student was not verified.
- Successive relearning for multi-step problems: no direct study found. Number of correct solutions needed for skill durability is unknown.

## Self-assessment: illusions of knowing, and how a "Mastered" tick should be earned

### Takeaway
Students systematically misjudge what works and what they know: fluent, massed, answer-visible study feels best and overpredicts performance. A Mastered tick shouldn't be a self-judgment; it should be earned by delayed, unaided, varied, markscheme-scored success and should be able to lapse.

### Cited Findings
- Koriat & Bjork 2005: judgments made with the answer visible overestimate future recall because learners fail to discount the present answer. Paired associates [EXTRAPOLATED] — [Koriat & Bjork 2005](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2005-koriat.pdf). The bias decreases with test experience and delayed judgment — [Koriat & Bjork 2006](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/illusions-of-competence-during-study-can-be-remedied-by-manipulat/)
- Kornell & Bjork 2009 stability bias, 12 experiments: learners underestimate learning from future study and assume memory stays stable, ignoring future forgetting [EXTRAPOLATED] — [Kornell & Bjork 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0017350)
- Karpicke 2009, foreign-language pairs: repeated retrieval after first correct recall .78, repeated study .39, dropping the item .35, but judgments of learning were .76, .87 and .75. Confidence was highest for one of the poorest conditions. When given control, learners tended to drop items once they could recall them [EXTRAPOLATED] — [Karpicke 2009](https://pcllab.github.io/downloads/2009/2009_Karpicke_JEPGeneral.pdf)
- Dunlosky & Rawson 2012, students self-scoring key-term definitions: better judgment accuracy predicted better retention, not explained by feedback, effort or trials to criterion. Overconfident learners stop too early. Later papers describe showing the student's response beside the correct idea units before self-scoring as the way accuracy was raised; I saw the abstract only and did not verify the exact procedure [NEAR] — [Dunlosky & Rawson 2012 record](https://scholarhub.vn/publication/Overconfidence-produces-underachievement-Inaccurate-self-evaluations-undermine-students-learning-and-retention/018938e3-b9b9-4d52-9bc1-a776d8c4a773)
- Rhodes & Tauber 2011 meta-analysis: delaying judgments of learning greatly increases relative accuracy, g = 0.93 (4,554 participants, 112 effects), with a very small direct memory benefit, g = 0.08 [EXTRAPOLATED, largely verbal] — [Rhodes & Tauber 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021705)
- Emeny et al. 2021, Year 7 mathematics: after massed practice, predictions were substantially overconfident (d = 0.92, 1.07 immediately after practice), but relatively accurate after spaced practice (d = 0.15, 0.23, ns). Directly relevant: spacing itself improves calibration [NEAR] — [Emeny 2021](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2021-emeny.pdf)
- Interleaving: Kornell & Bjork 2008 participants rated massing more effective even after their own test showed otherwise — [Kornell & Bjork 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02127.x). Samani & Pan physics students rated interleaved work harder and believed they learned less, despite better performance — [Samani & Pan 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8589969/)
- Kornell 2009 flashcards: spacing beat massing for 90% of participants, yet 72% initially believed massing better — [Kornell 2009](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/acp.1537)
- Deslauriers et al. 2019, randomised physics class comparison: active students learned more but felt they learned less, reading cognitive effort as poor learning. About instruction format, not scheduling [NEAR] — [Deslauriers 2019](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1821936116)
- Study behaviour: Hartwig & Dunlosky 2012 (324 undergraduates, correlational): self-testing and rereading both correlate with GPA, low performers study late, and all students but especially low performers are driven by deadlines — [Hartwig & Dunlosky 2012](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-011-0181-y). Karpicke, Butler & Roediger 2009 (177 undergraduates): self-testing rarely reported compared with rereading — [Karpicke, Butler & Roediger 2009](https://bpb-us-e2.wpmucdn.com/sites.wustl.edu/dist/8/805/files/2026/06/Karpicke-et-al.-2009-Metacognitive-strategies-in-student-learning-Do-students-practise-retrieval-when-they-study-on-thei.pdf)
- Review: people often have a faulty model of their own learning and misassess it from current performance and fluency — [Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell 2013](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143823)

### Inferences
Proposed rule for the app; each criterion is inferred from the above and the set has not been tested as a package.
- **Earned, not claimed.** A skill is Mastered only after:
  1. Correct, unaided attempts (no hint or answer visible before the attempt) in at least 3 separate sessions.
  2. The most recent at least about 1-2 weeks after the previous success, scaled down as the exam approaches.
  3. At least one on an unseen question variant.
  4. At least one inside a mixed set, not in a block of the same sub-topic.
- **Scoring.** Have the student type or photograph an attempt before reveal, then score point by point with the markscheme alongside. This is the Dunlosky & Rawson idea-unit approach. Reuse the app's existing tappable card rather than a single "got it" button.
- **Decay.** Show "Mastered, due for a check on [date]" and return to Practising on failure. Do not treat the tick as permanent, per stability bias.
- **Confidence capture, optional.** A 1-tap prediction before scoring, later plotted against actual results, could train calibration, but evidence of training benefit is not established here.
- **Choice architecture.** The app should choose the next item by default, as learners who are given control choose massing and dropping. Keep a "tell me why" note explaining why interleaved work feels harder, since students misread that.
- **Existing data.** `data/progress.json` must not be overwritten (project rule). Existing ticks should be treated as student-claimed; verified status should go in a new separate attempts log.

### Gaps
- Evidence on self-scoring accuracy for chemistry markschemes specifically was not found. Mark-point scoring is likely more objective than free definitions but is untested.
- No study tests a composite mastery criterion on later exam performance.

## Exam-countdown planning: scheduling when the exam is weeks or a few months away

### Takeaway
No trial directly compares countdown schedules for a chemistry exam, but the evidence converges: start reviews as early as possible, give every skill at least 2-3 spaced retrieval sessions, shrink gaps as the date approaches, shift to mixed practice, and cover widely before over-practising any one topic.

### Cited Findings
- Gap scaling with test delay, including reduction near the exam (7-day test about 3-day gap, 35-day about 8-day gap) [EXTRAPOLATED] — [Cepeda 2008](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)
- Spacing even in a short window works: 3 sessions 1 week apart vs massed, test 1 month later: d = 0.39-0.61 [NEAR] — [Emeny 2021](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2021-emeny.pdf)
- Spacing over amount under time limits: quiz spacing, not number of questions, improved pre-calculus retention [NEAR] — [ASEE paper](https://peer.asee.org/29900)
- Relearning is the high-yield element: large retention gains for minimal extra trials, and lower initial criterion matters less once relearning happens [NEAR] — [Rawson & Dunlosky 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023956); [Rawson & Dunlosky 2013](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030498)
- High-stakes classroom support: successive relearning d = 0.54-1.10 on exams [NEAR] — [Janes 2020 record](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/eric__EJ1265965)
- Modest time can matter: about 30 minutes weekly of model-chosen review gave +16.5% over massed [EXTRAPOLATED] — [Lindsey press release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/579380)
- Longer treatments have larger effects in Yang: single class g = 0.385, under a semester g = 0.521, a semester g = 0.547, longer g = 0.624, though this is correlational across studies — [Yang 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)
- Interleaving costs extra time per problem and pays off more at longer delays — [Rohrer 2019](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2019-rohrer.pdf)
- Compressed study creates misleading mastery and overconfidence, so a late-stage sense of readiness is unreliable — [Cepeda 2008](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf); [Emeny 2021](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2021-emeny.pdf)
- All students, and low performers especially, are driven by impending deadlines. In Samani & Pan most students crammed before midterms, and the authors suggest this may have obscured interleaving differences there — [Hartwig & Dunlosky 2012](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-011-0181-y); [Samani & Pan 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8589969/)

### Inferences
Proposed planner (my synthesis; proportions are judgement, not measured). `T` is days to exam, entered by the student, since the exam session is not recorded in these notes.

**Phases**

| Time left | New skills | Review/mixed | Mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| T over 120 | about 50% | about 50% | Example, small block, then mixed; gap about 10-15% of T, about 2-3 weeks |
| T 60-120 | about 30% | about 70% | Mostly short mixed sets; gap about 15-20% of T, about 10-14 days |
| T 21-60 | about 10% | about 90% | Mixed sets plus timed paper sections; gap about 20-25% of T, about 5-10 days |
| T under 21 | Triage only | Nearly all | Timed mixed papers and confusable-pair drills; gap about 3-5 days |

**Scheduler logic**
1. Compute how many spaced sessions fit: `n = floor(T / gap)`, capped at 3-4 per skill.
2. Prefer breadth: first give every high-frequency skill at least 2 spaced attempts before giving any skill a fourth. This follows from relearning's cost-effectiveness, but is not tested as such.
3. If time is short: drop initial reps first (criterion 3 to 1), not the later spaced session.
4. Priority score: overdue-ness × exam frequency × (1 − recent success), using `analysis/macro.py` statistics for frequency and examiner-reported weakness. The weighting is untested and should be easy to change.
5. Daily session template, about 45-60 minutes: about 10 minutes short recall (definitions, data-booklet facts), about 30 minutes mixed questions with confusable pairs adjacent, about 10 minutes correction against markscheme, then log and reschedule.
6. Include at least one full timed paper before the last fortnight as a calibration check, since self-prediction is unreliable.
7. Show "evidence of readiness" from delayed attempts only, not from same-session accuracy.

**Honest expected-benefit statement for the writer:** Combined effects are not additive and are not measured together. Rohrer's d = 0.83 already includes interleaving, spacing and retrieval in one package, so do not add separately reported effect sizes.

### Gaps
- No RCT compares countdown plans. Phase splits, session length and priority scores above are design judgement, not findings.
- Timing of final pre-exam review, and what to do in the last 24-48 hours, is not established in sources read.
- Full-text access limits: Adesope 2017, Rawson & Dunlosky 2022, Rawson et al. 2013, Latimier 2021, Hopkins 2016, Lyle 2020, Karpicke & Aue 2015, van Gog & Sweller 2015 and Gulacar 2022 were read only through abstracts or secondary summaries. Figures from those should be checked before any strong claim. The Chemistry row in Yang's Table 2 was re-extracted after an initial misaligned read; g = 0.340, k = 10 is the corrected value.
- Pan, Carpenter and other reviews after 2022 were not systematically searched. Newer classroom trials in chemistry may exist.
