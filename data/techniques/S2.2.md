## [ ] S2.2-06 · Explain molecular polarity: polar bonds plus non-cancelling geometry
Priority 1 · S2.2.5, S2.2.6 · Molecular polarity is flagged as difficult in 2009, 2012, 2013, 2015, 2024 and 2025; most candidates scored 0 or 1 of 2 on SO2 (25M.2.HL.6221.10).

**Step by step** (`25M.2.HL.6221.10`, 2 marks)

Sulfur dioxide, SO2, is a bent molecule with sulfur at the centre. Explain why SO2 is polar. [2]

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 5 s): Explain: give the cause, in two linked statements.
   - *command term*: Explain
   - *demands*: Why the bonds are polar and why their dipoles don't cancel.
   - *score mechanics*: Per point: M1 bond polarity, M2 non-cancellation from the shape.
   - *avoid*: Writing only 'SO2 is bent, so polar'.
   - *why*: Each statement is a separate marking point, so a single word ('bent') isn't an explanation.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 10 s): Polarity needs both bond polarity and molecular geometry.
   - *ao level*: AO3
   - *syllabus hook*: S2.2.6: molecular polarity depends on both bond polarity and molecular geometry.
   - *textbook gap*: Books show arrows cancelling in CO2; exams want the reason in words, linked to the actual shape.
   - *avoid*: Explaining polarity only by the lone pair on S (25M.2.SL.TZ3.7).
   - *why*: The syllabus statement joins two ideas, and the markscheme gives one mark to each.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 10 s): SO2 looks like CO2, but the lone pair bends it.
   - *disguise*: The formula SO2 looks like CO2, which is non-polar.
   - *heuristic*: Check both: polar bonds AND an asymmetric shape. Both are needed for a polar molecule.
   - *avoid*: Calling SO2 non-polar by analogy with CO2.
   - *why*: In CO2 the two dipoles are at 180° and cancel; in bent SO2 they add to a net dipole.
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 5 s): Rule out δ− on S and 'difference too small'.
   - *definitely wrong*: S given as the δ− atom: O is more electronegative than S.; 'Bonds non-polar: difference too small': Any S–O difference makes the bonds polar.; Lone pair alone as the reason: Must say bond dipoles don't cancel.
   - *avoid*: Thinking polar bonds need a large difference.
   - *why*: O is more electronegative than S, and any difference makes the bond polar.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 10 s): Section 9: compare the electronegativities of S and O.
   - *sections*: 9
   - *pattern*: Section 9 lists electronegativity for each element; read S and O.
   - *memorise*: The rule that symmetric shapes cancel bond dipoles is NOT in the booklet.
   - *avoid*: Assuming the central atom is δ−.
   - *why*: The more electronegative atom takes δ−, so the booklet decides where the charges go.
6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 40 s): Two sentences and a δ-labelled sketch are enough.
   - *workflow*: EN difference: bonds polar; Shape decides cancelling; Label δ+ and δ−
   - *fat to trim*: Skip electronegativity numbers; saying O is more electronegative is enough.
   - *avoid*: Redrawing resonance structures.
   - *why*: Both marks need ideas, not length.
7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 15 s): M1 bonds polar; M2 dipoles don't cancel at less than 180°.
   - *trigger phrases*: bonds are polar / electronegativity difference; bond dipoles do not cancel / net dipole; bonds at an angle less than 180°
   - *trap boundary*: Linking the dipole to the S lone pair alone gets no M2; δ− on S is wrong.
   - *delta*: Most scored one or zero; full credit needs the geometry reason for non-cancellation.
   - *evidence ref*: 25M.2.HL.6221.10
   - *avoid*: Putting δ− on S, or saying the EN difference is too small (25M.2.HL.6221.10 examiner).
   - *why*: M2 also accepts an unsymmetrical charge distribution, or δ+ on S and δ− on both O. ECF comes from the geometry you gave.

**Answer:** M1: the S–O bonds are polar because O is more electronegative than S. M2: the molecule is bent (angle less than 180°), so the bond dipoles don't cancel and there is a net dipole; S is δ+ and both O atoms are δ−. (Polar S–O bonds at an angle: dipoles don't cancel, so net dipole.)
**Try next:** `25M.1A.HL.6213.7`

## [ ] S2.2-08 · Hydrogen bond: H on N/O/F attracted to another molecule's N/O/F
Priority 1 · S2.2.8 · May 2025 flags a wrong concept of H-bonds; on 25M.2.HL.6221.7 only 57% annotated the bond and many failed to outline it.

**Step by step** (`25M.2.HL.6221.7`, 2 marks)

Chains of nylon 6,6 contain N–H groups and C=O groups in their amide links. Hydrogen bonding between neighbouring chains makes the polymer strong. Outline how a hydrogen bond forms. [2]

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 5 s): Outline: a brief account of the two features.
   - *command term*: Outline
   - *demands*: The two features of a hydrogen bond, briefly.
   - *score mechanics*: Per point, two marks; a labelled diagram can earn both.
   - *avoid*: Writing about nylon's uses or strength.
   - *why*: Outline needs no detail on strength; two precise statements earn both marks.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 5 s): The syllabus defines the hydrogen bond in two parts; marks follow.
   - *ao level*: AO2
   - *syllabus hook*: S2.2.8: hydrogen bonds occur when H, covalently bonded to an electronegative atom, attracts a neighbouring electronegative atom.
   - *textbook gap*: Books show water; exams test the definition on polymers and amides.
   - *avoid*: Describing a hydrogen bond as a covalent bond (25M.2.HL.6221.7 examiner).
   - *why*: The guidance defines it as H covalently bonded to an electronegative atom attracting a neighbouring electronegative atom, which matches M1 and M2.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 10 s): A long polymer formula hides a simple N–H···O=C link.
   - *disguise*: A big repeating polymer structure instead of small molecules.
   - *heuristic*: Circle every H on N, O or F, then every N, O or F on the other molecule.
   - *avoid*: Drawing hydrogen bonds within one chain (25M.2.HL.6221.7 examiner).
   - *why*: Only the amide groups matter; the CH2 chains can't hydrogen bond.
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 5 s): C–H hydrogens and same-chain links are out.
   - *definitely wrong*: Hydrogen bond from a C–H hydrogen: H must be bonded to N, O or F.; Hydrogen bond drawn within one chain: The question asks for one between chains.; Calling it a covalent bond: A hydrogen bond is an intermolecular attraction.
   - *avoid*: Joining H of CH2 to O.
   - *why*: Hydrogen bonding needs H on N, O or F, and the question asks for links between chains.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 5 s): Section 9: N, O, F electronegativities far exceed H.
   - *sections*: 9
   - *pattern*: Section 9 electronegativity: F, O, N are the highest values.
   - *memorise*: The definition of a hydrogen bond is NOT in the booklet.
   - *avoid*: Including Cl or C as hydrogen-bonding atoms.
   - *why*: The large difference makes H δ+ enough to be attracted to a lone pair.
6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 40 s): One sentence each for donor and acceptor.
   - *workflow*: Find H on N/O/F; Find acceptor on neighbour; Dotted line between them
   - *fat to trim*: Skip bond strength values and property consequences.
   - *avoid*: Explaining electronegativity from scratch.
   - *why*: The markscheme has exactly two points, so stop after them.
7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 15 s): M1 H bonded to electronegative atom; M2 attracted to neighbour's.
   - *trigger phrases*: H covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom; attracted to the electronegative atom of a neighbouring molecule
   - *trap boundary*: Only describing the polar N–H bond stops at M1; the attraction must be to another molecule.
   - *delta*: M1 is the δ+ hydrogen; M2 is its attraction to an N, O or F on a different chain.
   - *evidence ref*: 25M.2.HL.6221.7
   - *avoid*: Not fully outlining formation between molecules, the common failure (25M.2.HL.6221.7 examiner).
   - *why*: 'F, O or N' is accepted for 'electronegative atom', and a labelled diagram can earn both marks.

**Answer:** M1: a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom (here N; F, O or N accepted) is δ+. M2: it is attracted to an electronegative atom (the O of C=O) on a neighbouring chain or molecule. A labelled diagram can earn both marks. (δ+ H on N/O/F attracts an N/O/F atom on another molecule.)

## [ ] S2.2-02 · Explain a shape: count domains and lone pairs on the central atom
Priority 2 · S2.2.4, S2.2.1 · May 2025 reports flag explaining geometry with electron domains as difficult; SL average 1.04/2 on the HCN geometry question (25M.2.SL.TZ2.1).

**Step by step** (`25M.2.HL.6215.1`, 2 marks)

Hydrogen cyanide, HCN, is a volatile liquid; H is bonded to C, and C is bonded to N. State the molecular geometry of HCN and explain it without using hybridization. [2]
