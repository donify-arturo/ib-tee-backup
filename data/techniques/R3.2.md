## [ ] R3.2-06 · E°cell: right size, right sign
Priority 1 · R3.2.13 · The pack's statistics list 19% correct, but the report says 59% scored and 19% had the right value with the wrong sign. Standard cell potential was flagged as difficult in May 2025.

**Step by step** (`25M.1A.HL.6213.37`, 1 marks)

A voltaic cell joins a Ag(s)/Ag+(aq) half-cell to a Mg(s)/Mg2+(aq) half-cell with a salt bridge and voltmeter.
Ag+(aq) + e− ⇌ Ag(s) E° = +0.80 V
Mg2+(aq) + 2e− ⇌ Mg(s) E° = −2.37 V
What is E°cell?
A. −3.17 V
B. −1.57 V
C. +1.57 V
D. +3.17 V

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 3 s): No command term: select the value with correct sign.
   - *command term*: none
   - *demands*: Select the correct value of E°cell, including its sign.
   - *score mechanics*: One mark, all or nothing.
   - *avoid*: Picking the first option showing the right digits.
   - *why*: All options use the same two numbers, so the choice tests your method and sign.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 5 s): E°cell from two reduction potentials; sign shows spontaneity.
   - *ao level*: AO2
   - *syllabus hook*: R3.2.13: E°cell is calculated from standard electrode potentials and is positive for a spontaneous reaction.
   - *textbook gap*: Books drill the subtraction. This exam tests the meaning: both signs of the same size appear as options.
   - *avoid*: Treating the sign as a matter of order of subtraction.
   - *why*: A voltaic cell runs by a spontaneous reaction, and that is what a positive E°cell means.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 5 s): Different electron numbers tempt you into scaling.
   - *disguise*: Coefficients of 1e− and 2e− suggest that the silver value should be doubled.
   - *heuristic*: Coefficient scaling belongs to equations and ΔG°, never to E° values.
   - *avoid*: Doubling 0.80 because Mg transfers two electrons.
   - *why*: E° is intensive: it describes a potential per electron, so it does not change with the coefficient in an equation.
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 5 s): Negative options fail; gap must exceed 2.37.
   - *definitely wrong*: A: A negative value contradicts a working voltaic cell.; B: A negative value contradicts a working voltaic cell.; C: Values straddling zero must differ by more than 2.37 V.
   - *avoid*: Adding signed values, 0.80 + (−2.37), which gives the 1.57 distractors.
   - *why*: A voltaic cell's reaction is spontaneous, so negative options go. Values on either side of zero must differ by more than either value alone.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 3 s): Values given here; section 19 lists them otherwise.
   - *sections*: 19
   - *pattern*: Section 19 lists reductions from most negative to most positive; the lower one is the cathode.
   - *memorise*: NOT in the booklet: E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode, and that positive means spontaneous.
   - *avoid*: Reading the wrong line, reported for copper in 03M.2.HL.TZ0.7.
   - *why*: Written questions direct you to section 19, and a misread line gives a wrong value.
6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 15 s): Larger minus smaller; values straddle zero, so add.
   - *workflow*: Cathode: more positive E°; Cathode minus anode; Check sign is positive
   - *fat to trim*: Skip the balanced overall equation; E°cell needs only two numbers.
   - *avoid*: Writing out the full cell equation.
   - *why*: Values on either side of zero always give a gap equal to the sum of their sizes.
7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 5 s): Only +3.17 V scores; −3.17 V earns nothing.
   - *trigger phrases*: +3.17 V; positive for a spontaneous cell
   - *trap boundary*: A correct magnitude is not enough: sign is part of the answer.
   - *delta*: D keeps the positive sign; A is the same arithmetic with the sign reversed.
   - *evidence ref*: 25M.1A.HL.6213.37
   - *avoid*: Choosing −3.17 V: 19% got the value with the wrong sign (25M.1A.HL.6213.37 report).
   - *why*: Same magnitude with a wrong sign shows no grasp that a voltaic cell is spontaneous, and MCQs have no partial credit.

**Answer:** D. E°cell = +0.80 − (−2.37) = +3.17 V. (E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode, and a working cell's value is positive.)

## [ ] R3.2-13 · Aqueous electrolysis: water competes
Priority 1 · R3.2.15 · Mean 0.94/2, and 46% on the MCQ form. Flagged as difficult across May 2012, 2014, 2015, 2017 and 2025.

**Step by step** (`24N.2.HL.TZ0.3`, 2 marks)

Dilute KCl(aq) is electrolysed using inert electrodes, as one of two cells connected in series. State the half-equation at the anode (positive electrode) and at the cathode (negative electrode). Use section 19 of the data booklet. [2]

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 3 s): State: write two equations; no explanation needed.
   - *command term*: State
   - *demands*: Give one half-equation for each named electrode.
   - *score mechanics*: One mark per electrode; [1 max] if both correct equations are at the wrong electrode.
   - *avoid*: Explaining the E° comparison at length for two marks.
   - *why*: State asks for a brief answer, so marks sit in formula and electrode, not in argument.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 5 s): Water competes at both electrodes in aqueous electrolysis.
   - *ao level*: AO3
   - *syllabus hook*: R3.2.15: deduce products of aqueous electrolysis from standard electrode potentials.
   - *textbook gap*: Books teach concentrated NaCl. Here dilute solution reverses the anode product, and a data lookup is expected.
   - *avoid*: Answering as if the solution were molten KCl.
   - *why*: Water can be reduced and oxidized, so it is a third competitor at each electrode, unlike in a melt.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 5 s): KCl replaces the usual NaCl; series wiring is irrelevant.
   - *disguise*: Two cells in series and a less common salt make this look unusual.
   - *heuristic*: Water in, compare everything. Check dilute or concentrated, then decide each electrode separately.
   - *avoid*: Thinking the copper sulfate cell alters the KCl chemistry.
   - *why*: Potassium behaves like sodium here. The cells in series share current, not chemistry.
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 5 s): No potassium metal, and no chlorine in dilute solution.
   - *definitely wrong*: K+ + e− → K at the cathode: K+ has a far more negative E° than water.; 2Cl− → Cl2 + 2e− at the anode here: Marked wrong for this dilute solution.; Oxidation written at the cathode: The cathode is always reduction.
   - *avoid*: Giving potassium at the cathode. Magnesium was reported similarly (12M.2.HL.TZ1.4).
   - *why*: K+ is far more negative than water, and the markscheme rejects chlorine for this dilute solution.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 15 s): Section 19 ranks K+, water, O2 and Cl2.
   - *sections*: 19
   - *pattern*: Cathode: higher E° wins. Anode: lower E° wins, and read each line backwards.
   - *memorise*: NOT in the booklet: concentration effect, that concentrated chloride gives chlorine.
   - *avoid*: Citing old section numbers printed on legacy papers.
   - *why*: K+ −2.93 V loses to water −0.83 V. O2/H2O +1.23 V is just below Cl2/Cl− +1.36 V, so water is favoured.
6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 50 s): Cathode hydrogen, anode oxygen, then write with electrons.
   - *workflow*: Water competes at both; Dilute: O2 at anode; Reactive cation: H2 forms; Check electron side
   - *fat to trim*: Skip Cell 1 and any overall equation; only Cell 2 is asked for.
   - *avoid*: Going through every species in section 19.
   - *why*: After you know the dilute rule, this needs a quick check of E° then two lines.
7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 10 s): Oxygen at anode, hydrogen at cathode; chlorine scores zero.
   - *trigger phrases*: H2O → ½O2 + 2H+ + 2e− or 2OH− → ½O2 + H2O + 2e−; H2O + e− → ½H2 + OH− or H+ + e− → ½H2
   - *trap boundary*: Reactions producing chlorine earn no mark. Correct equations at wrong electrodes get [1 max]. Reversible arrows are ignored.
   - *delta*: [1 max] when equations are swapped. Two marks need oxygen formation at anode and hydrogen formation at cathode.
   - *evidence ref*: 24N.2.HL.TZ0.3
   - *avoid*: Writing chlorine's half-equation: awareness was only about half (24N.2.HL.TZ0.3 report).
   - *why*: Each electrode's mark needs a water-based or OH−/H+ equation. The dilute solution removes chloride as the accepted anode product.

**Answer:** Anode: H2O(l) → ½O2 + 2H+(aq) + 2e− (or 2OH−(aq) → ½O2 + H2O(l) + 2e−) [1]. Cathode: H2O(l) + e− → ½H2(g) + OH−(aq) (or H+(aq) + e− → ½H2(g)) [1]. (Compare ions with water by E°; dilute chloride and potassium ions both lose.)

## [ ] R3.2-01 · Oxidation states name the agents
Priority 2 · R3.2.1 · Easy on a single state (89%) but harder on roles (about 65%). 'Oxidation numbers', 'reducing agent' and 'oxidizing agent' are all frequent error signals.

**Step by step** (`25N.1A.HL.6219.33`, 1 marks)

Hypochlorite ions react with sulfur dioxide in water:
ClO−(aq) + SO2(g) + H2O(l) → Cl−(aq) + SO4 2−(aq) + 2H+(aq)
Which pair gives the oxidizing agent first, then the reducing agent?
A. ClO− and H2O
B. SO2 and SO4 2−
C. SO2 and ClO−
D. ClO− and SO2

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 3 s): No command term: choose the row with both roles right.
   - *command term*: none
   - *demands*: Select the row with the oxidizing agent and reducing agent in the correct columns.
   - *score mechanics*: One mark, all or nothing.
   - *avoid*: Stopping once you have found the oxidizing agent.
   - *why*: Each row tests both roles at once, so one correct half gains nothing.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 5 s): Naming agents needs oxidation states, not just spotting oxygen.
   - *ao level*: AO2
   - *syllabus hook*: R3.2.1: identify oxidized and reduced species and the oxidizing and reducing agents.
   - *textbook gap*: Books practise definitions on simple metal reactions. This item uses an oxoanion with water present, so states must be assigned.
   - *avoid*: Judging by oxygen alone when three reactants contain or supply it.
   - *why*: Oxygen appears in several species here, so oxygen gain or loss is hard to read. Oxidation states work on every redox equation.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 10 s): Rare hypochlorite hides a simple Cl +1 to −1 change.
   - *disguise*: Hypochlorite is rarely met, and three reactants suggest a complicated change.
   - *heuristic*: Ignore names. Mark only atoms whose state changes, then read off the two reactants holding them.
   - *avoid*: Trying to balance half-equations because the species look unfamiliar.
   - *why*: Only two atoms change state. Everything else is bookkeeping that balances the equation.
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 5 s): B names a product; water in A does not change.
   - *definitely wrong*: B: SO4 2− is a product, and agents are reactants.; A: H and O in water keep their states, so water is not the reducing agent.
   - *avoid*: Picking B because sulfur visibly changes in it.
   - *why*: An agent must be present before the reaction to act on another species, and a species with unchanged states is not an agent.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 2 s): No booklet help: oxidation state rules must be known.
   - *sections*: 
   - *pattern*: No table applies; use the rules directly.
   - *memorise*: NOT in the booklet: O is usually −2, H usually +1, and states sum to the species' charge.
   - *avoid*: Searching section 19 for hypochlorite.
   - *why*: Section 19 lists reduction potentials, but ClO− is absent and oxidation state rules are not given.
6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 20 s): Assign states to Cl and S only, then choose.
   - *workflow*: Assign Cl and S states; Falls: oxidizing agent; Rises: reducing agent
   - *fat to trim*: Skip half-equations and electron counts; two oxidation states settle this.
   - *avoid*: Writing out and balancing two half-equations for a one-mark item.
   - *why*: H stays +1 and O stays −2 in every species, so checking them adds no information.
7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 5 s): Only ClO− oxidizing with SO2 reducing earns the mark.
   - *trigger phrases*: ClO− is the oxidizing agent; SO2 is the reducing agent
   - *trap boundary*: The row must be right in both columns: C holds the correct species in reversed roles and scores nothing.
   - *delta*: D names the reactant holding each changing atom. C swaps the roles; B names a product.
   - *evidence ref*: 25N.1A.HL.6219.33
   - *avoid*: Reversing the two agents, reported in 07M.2.HL.TZ0.3 and 13M.2.HL.TZ1.4.
   - *why*: The agent is named after what it does to the other species, so it undergoes the opposite change itself.

## [ ] R3.2-05 · What changes while a voltaic cell runs
Priority 2 · R3.2.5, R3.2.6 · Repeatedly hard: 39% (11M), 43% (25M) and 48% (12M SL) on electrode and ion questions; 64% on the worked item.

**Step by step** (`24N.1.HL.TZ0.29`, 1 marks)

Two half-cells, metal Z in Z2+(aq) and metal X in X2+(aq), are joined by a voltmeter and a salt bridge.
X2+(aq) + 2e− ⇌ X(s) E° = +0.25 V
Z2+(aq) + 2e− ⇌ Z(s) E° = −0.60 V
Which change happens while this cell produces electricity?
A. Positive ions in the salt bridge move to the Z half-cell.
B. Electrons move from the X half-cell to the Z half-cell.
C. The X2+ concentration rises.
D. The Z electrode loses mass.

   - *command term*: none
   - *demands*: Select the single true statement about a working cell.

   - *avoid*: Stopping to explain why the others fail once D is proved.
   - *why*: Each statement is independently true or false, so one correct check is enough.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 5 s): One E° comparison predicts every change in the cell.
   - *ao level*: AO2

   - *avoid*: Treating each option as a separate fact to recall.
   - *why*: Once the anode is known, electron flow, ion flow, mass changes and concentrations all follow.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 5 s): Letters X and Z hide which metal is the anode.
   - *disguise*: Unnamed metals, and options describing different particles and quantities.
   - *heuristic*: Write Z → Z2+ + 2e− on the page, and test each option against this one line.

   - *avoid*: Looking for metals X and Z in the table.

   - *trap boundary*: The other three options each reverse one direction: ions, electrons or concentration.
