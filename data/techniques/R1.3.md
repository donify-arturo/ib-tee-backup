## [ ] R1.3-03 · Incomplete combustion: hydrogen always ends as water, never H2
Priority 1 · R1.3.2 · 59% correct on 16N with 31% choosing H2. 12N was the hardest item on its paper; H2 misconception again in 24M.

**Step by step** (`25N.1A.HL.6219.22`, 1 marks)

Propane burns with too little oxygen (incomplete combustion). Which products may form?
A. CO2 and H2 only
B. CO, C and H2
C. CO2, H2O and H2
D. CO2, CO and C

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 5 s): MCQ: pick everything that could form.
   - *command term*: none
   - *demands*: Choose the set of products that may form.
   - *score mechanics*: 1 mark, all or nothing.
   - *avoid*: Rejecting D because CO2 also forms in complete combustion.
   - *why*: The 12N report noted that 'could' and 'limited oxygen' wording tripped many students. Read the stem for possibility, not certainty (12N.1.SL.TZ0.29).
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 5 s): AO1: recall the products of incomplete combustion.
   - *ao level*: AO1
   - *syllabus hook*: R1.3.2: incomplete combustion of organic compounds gives carbon monoxide and carbon.
   - *textbook gap*: Books show one tidy equation making CO. Exams mix CO2, CO and C, and add H2 as bait.
   - *avoid*: Thinking 'incomplete' means the fuel splits back into its elements, including H2.
   - *why*: R1.3.2 names carbon monoxide and carbon. Exams test it by offering hydrogen as a tempting extra product.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 10 s): Soot on glassware or a yellow flame means incomplete combustion.
   - *disguise*: Soot on a crucible during heating, 'limited air' wording, or burning plastics.
   - *heuristic*: Black deposit or smoky flame: carbon from too little oxygen.
   - *avoid*: Blaming soot on the crucible burning (16N.3.HL.TZ0.2).
   - *why*: A practical question may never say 'incomplete combustion'. A black deposit from a closed air hole is carbon, and it changes the mass readings (16N.3.SL.TZ0.2).
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 5 s): A, B and C all contain H2.
   - *definitely wrong*: A: Contains H2.; B: Contains H2.; C: Contains H2.
   - *avoid*: Keeping C because it also has water.
   - *why*: Hydrogen in a fuel is oxidised to water even when oxygen is scarce, so H2 is never a combustion product.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 5 s): Section 14: CO and C still have energy to release.
   - *sections*: 14
   - *pattern*: Section 14 rows for CO(g) −283 and C(graphite) −394 kJ mol−1.
   - *memorise*: The products of incomplete combustion are NOT in the booklet.
   - *avoid*: Thinking the booklet lists incomplete combustion products.
   - *why*: CO (−283) and carbon (−394) both have combustion enthalpies in section 14. Making them instead of CO2 wastes the fuel's energy.
6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 10 s): Cross out every H2 option: done in 10 seconds.
   - *workflow*: Hydrogen always becomes water; Carbon becomes CO2, CO, C; Cross out any H2
   - *fat to trim*: No balancing: this is recognising products.
   - *avoid*: Writing and balancing incomplete-combustion equations for each option.
   - *why*: Three of the four options contain H2. One rule removes them without any balancing.
7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 5 s): D only: CO2, CO and C can all appear.
   - *trigger phrases*: carbon monoxide / CO; carbon / C / soot
   - *trap boundary*: H2 is never credited. Naming 'incomplete combustion' as a condition doesn't answer a products question (14M.2.SL.TZ2.3).
   - *delta*: Right: every H goes to H2O. Tempting: H2 listed as a product.
   - *evidence ref*: 21M.2.SL.TZ1.5
   - *avoid*: Choosing an H2 option: 31% thought H2 and CO2 form (16N.1.HL.TZ0.27); same error in 24M.1.SL.TZ1.27.
   - *why*: 'May form' means any product that is possible. In limited oxygen all three carbon products are possible, and water always forms.

**Answer:** D. With limited oxygen, carbon may form CO2, CO and C (soot). Hydrogen forms water, never H2. (H always becomes water; carbon splits between CO2, CO and soot.)

## [ ] R1.3-05 · Photosynthesis equation: the carbon fixation behind biofuels
Priority 1 · R1.3.4 · Only about half scored it in 25M HL (the question overall was 40%). Flagged again in 25M SL TZ3.

**Step by step** (`25M.2.HL.6221.9`, 1 marks)

Ethanol biofuel is made from glucose, which plants produce by photosynthesis (biological fixation of carbon). Part (a): state the balanced equation for photosynthesis. [1]

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 5 s): 'State': give the equation, no explanation.
   - *command term*: State
   - *demands*: A balanced symbol equation.
   - *score mechanics*: 1 mark, all or nothing.
   - *avoid*: Writing a word equation only.
   - *why*: 'State' wants a brief specific answer. Writing words around the equation earns nothing extra.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 5 s): AO1: recall a balanced equation named in the guidance.
   - *ao level*: AO1
   - *syllabus hook*: R1.3.4: biofuels come from carbon fixed by photosynthesis; its reactants and products should be known.
   - *textbook gap*: Biology teaches it in words. Chemistry wants a balanced equation with the correct glucose formula.
   - *avoid*: Assuming it's biology-only and not revising it.
   - *why*: R1.3.4 guidance says the reactants and products of photosynthesis should be known. That makes it a recall mark many students lose.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 10 s): 'Why is a biofuel carbon neutral?' uses this equation.
   - *disguise*: Asked how biofuels reduce net CO2, or what 'biological fixation of carbon' means.
   - *heuristic*: Biofuel carbon came from the air through photosynthesis, so burning only returns it.
   - *avoid*: Saying biofuels release no CO2 when burned.
   - *why*: 19N.3.SL.TZ0.14 credits saying the biofuel's raw material used up CO2 when it formed, with this equation. Burning returns that CO2.
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 5 s): O2 on the left means you wrote respiration.
   - *definitely wrong*: An equation with O2 as a reactant: That is respiration, not photosynthesis.; Any equation without O2: The oxygen atoms cannot balance without O2 as a product.
   - *avoid*: Leaving O2 out to make balancing easier.
   - *why*: Photosynthesis releases oxygen. If O2 is a reactant, you have written cellular respiration, which examiners saw often.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 5 s): Nothing in the booklet: learn it.
   - *sections*: 
   - *pattern*: None.
   - *memorise*: The photosynthesis equation and glucose formula C6H12O6 are NOT in the booklet.
   - *avoid*: Wasting time searching sections for glucose.
   - *why*: The booklet has no biochemical equations, so the only route is memory.
6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 20 s): Write it from memory in 20 seconds.
   - *workflow*: Glucose and oxygen come out; Sixes everywhere else
   - *fat to trim*: State symbols are optional extras here; no explanation needed.
   - *avoid*: Deriving glucose's formula from scratch.
   - *why*: This is recall: no derivation is needed, and the 18-oxygen check takes seconds.
7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 5 s): One mark for the full balanced equation.
   - *trigger phrases*: 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2
   - *trap boundary*: Respiration (glucose + O2 → CO2 + H2O) scores zero.
   - *delta*: Zero vs one mark: every species present and every 6 in place.
   - *evidence ref*: 25M.2.HL.6221.9
   - *avoid*: Unbalanced equation, missing O2, reverse reaction or wrong glucose formula: all reported (25M.2.HL.6221.9).
   - *why*: There is no partial credit: one wrong formula or coefficient loses the only mark.

**Answer:** 6CO2(g) + 6H2O(l) → C6H12O6(aq) + 6O2(g) [1]. (6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2: respiration written backwards.)

## [ ] R1.3-01 · Balancing alcohol combustion: subtract the fuel's own oxygen
Priority 2 · R1.3.1 · 77% correct on 25M SL (D the main distractor). 'Balanced equation' is a repeated error signal in reports.

**Step by step** (`25M.1A.HL.6213.20`, 1 marks)

Ethanol, C2H5OH, burns completely in oxygen to form carbon dioxide and water. The equation is balanced using the smallest whole numbers. What is the coefficient of O2?
A. 1
B. 2
C. 3
D. 7

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 5 s): No command term: one mark for the correct letter.
   - *command term*: none
   - *demands*: Balance the whole equation, then report the O2 coefficient.
   - *score mechanics*: 1 mark, all or nothing.
   - *avoid*: Picking the first option that matches a count you made halfway through.
   - *why*: No working is marked. Write the balance quickly in the margin and read off one coefficient.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 10 s): AO2: write a combustion equation for an alcohol.
   - *ao level*: AO2
   - *syllabus hook*: R1.3.1: deduce equations for combustion reactions, including hydrocarbons and alcohols.
   - *textbook gap*: Books mostly practise alkanes. The exam swaps in an alcohol, which changes the oxygen count.
   - *avoid*: Treating ethanol like a hydrocarbon and ignoring the O in OH.
   - *why*: The R1.3.1 skill names alcohols specifically. The oxygen already in the fuel is the twist examiners use to separate students who balance mechanically.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 10 s): Any fuel containing O: subtract its oxygen before halving.
   - *disguise*: The fuel contains O (an alcohol or ether) or another element (S, Pb) that also takes oxygen.
   - *heuristic*: List every element in the fuel. Each one needs a product, and the fuel's O counts on the left.
   - *avoid*: Leaving out a product for an extra element, such as PbO2 from PbC8H20 (12M.2.SL.TZ1.2).
   - *why*: The method is the same for ethers, sugars or fuels containing Pb or S. Each extra element needs its own oxide product, then the O count is done last.
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 5 s): A is too small for two carbons; D is a count of atoms.
   - *definitely wrong*: A: One O2 cannot even supply the oxygen for 2 CO2.; D: 7 is the product O-atom total. It ignores ethanol's O and is atoms, not molecules.
   - *avoid*: Picking D because 7 appears in your working.
   - *why*: Two carbons need 4 O atoms just for the CO2. One O2 gives only 2, so A fails before you even count water.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 5 s): Section 14 gives alcohol formulas if only a name appears.
   - *sections*: 14
   - *pattern*: Section 14 table: formula column for alcohols such as C3H7OH and C4H9OH.
   - *memorise*: The products of complete combustion (CO2, H2O) and how to balance are NOT in the booklet.
   - *avoid*: Looking for combustion products or balancing rules in the booklet.
   - *why*: Section 14 lists propan-1-ol C3H7OH and butan-1-ol C4H9OH with states, which saves deriving formulas from names.
6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 40 s): C, then H, then O last: under 40 seconds.
   - *workflow*: Carbons make CO2; Hydrogens halve into water; Subtract the fuel's own O; Halve what's left for O2
   - *fat to trim*: No doubling needed: ethanol already gives whole numbers.
   - *avoid*: Starting with oxygen, which forces you to redo every coefficient.
   - *why*: C and H each sit in only one product, so they set the right side. Oxygen appears everywhere, so it is balanced last.
7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 5 s): One mark for the letter: only C (3) scores.
   - *trigger phrases*: smallest whole numbers; complete combustion gives CO2 + H2O
   - *trap boundary*: 7 comes from supplying all 7 product O atoms from O2. It ignores ethanol's own O.
   - *delta*: Right: 4 + 3 = 7 O atoms on the right, minus 1 from ethanol = 6, so 3 O2. Tempting: all 7 from O2.
   - *evidence ref*: 25M.1A.HL.6213.20
   - *avoid*: Choosing D: the most common error was not counting ethanol's oxygen atom (25M.1A.SL.TZ2.17).
   - *why*: MCQs are all or nothing. Each distractor is the number you get from one slip in the oxygen count.

**Answer:** C. C2H5OH + 3O2 → 2CO2 + 3H2O: 7 product O atoms minus ethanol's 1 leaves 6, which is 3 O2. (Balance C, then H, then O last, after subtracting the fuel's own O.)
**Try next:** `25N.1A.HL.6219.19`

## [ ] R1.3-07 · Combustion enthalpy from bond enthalpies: count every bond, broken minus formed
Priority 2 · R1.3.1 · About 40% full marks on 25M HL (b)(ii), the hardest R1.3 item in macro data (40% correct).

**Step by step** (`25M.2.HL.6221.9`, 3 marks)

Part (b)(ii): calculate the enthalpy change of combustion of ethanol, in kJ mol−1, using average bond enthalpies (data booklet section 12).
C2H5OH(l) + 3O2(g) → 2CO2(g) + 3H2O(g)
Bond enthalpies / kJ mol−1: C–C 346, C–H 414, C–O 358, O–H 463, O=O 498, C=O 804. [3]

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 5 s): 'Calculate': show the stages; final answer can carry all.
   - *command term*: Calculate
   - *demands*: A numerical ΔH with working shown.
   - *score mechanics*: 3 marks: bonds broken, bonds formed, final ΔH. A correct final answer scores 3.
   - *avoid*: Writing only a final number with no sums.
   - *why*: 'Calculate' needs working. If the final number is wrong, the shown broken and formed sums still earn marks.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 10 s): AO2: bond enthalpy calculation applied to a fuel.
   - *ao level*: AO2
   - *syllabus hook*: R1.3.1: combustion of organic compounds; enthalpy here by the R1.2 bond enthalpy method.
   - *textbook gap*: Books use small molecules. The exam uses a fuel with C–O and O–H, and water as gas.
   - *avoid*: Using molecular formula C2H6O instead of the structure.
   - *why*: R1.3 sets the bond enthalpy method from R1.2 on a fuel. A molecule with five different bond types tests careful counting.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 10 s): Any fuel: draw its structure before counting.
   - *disguise*: A different fuel, or energy per gram of ethanol vs octane (09M.2.SL.TZ2.6).
   - *heuristic*: Structure first, then a table of bond counts. Molecular formulas hide C–O and O–H.
   - *avoid*: Assuming the bond-enthalpy value should match section 14.
   - *why*: Bond enthalpies are averages for gases, so −1263 differs from the measured −1367 used later in the question. Expect a 'why different' follow-up.
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 5 s): Positive ΔH for combustion is wrong.
   - *definitely wrong*: Any positive ΔH: Combustion is always exothermic.; A total using 6 C–H: Ethanol has only 5 C–H; the sixth H is on O.
   - *avoid*: Keeping +1263 because the arithmetic looks clean.
   - *why*: Burning a fuel always releases energy, so any positive value shows a subtraction or counting error.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 10 s): Section 12, as the question directs, gives the bond values.
   - *sections*: 12
   - *pattern*: Section 12 bond enthalpy table (as directed by the question).
   - *memorise*: Ethanol's structure and bond counts are NOT in the booklet; learn 'broken minus formed'.
   - *avoid*: Using the C=O value meant for another type of compound.
   - *why*: The question points to section 12 for average bond enthalpies. Use the C=O value given for CO2 (804).
6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 120 s): Bond table, two sums, one subtraction: 2 minutes.
   - *workflow*: Draw it, count bonds; Break minus make; Negative for combustion
   - *fat to trim*: Don't convert to kJ g−1 or compare with section 14 unless asked.
   - *avoid*: Breaking the CO2 and H2O bonds as well.
   - *why*: A small table of bond, count and value prevents missed bonds. It also gives the examiner your working marks.
7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 10 s): Broken 4731, formed 5994, ΔH −1263: [3].
   - *trigger phrases*: 346 + 5(414) + 358 + 463 + 3(498) = 4731; 4(804) + 6(463) = 5994; −1263 kJ mol−1
   - *trap boundary*: Award [3] for the correct final answer. +1263 has the wrong sign and loses the final mark.
   - *delta*: Partial: correct sums but wrong order or sign. Full: −1263 kJ mol−1.
   - *evidence ref*: 25M.2.HL.6221.9
   - *avoid*: Subtracting the wrong way round (25M.2.HL.6221.9 examiner).
   - *why*: Marks go to the broken sum, the formed sum and the final value. A correct final answer alone earns all three.

**Answer:** Broken: 346 + 5(414) + 358 + 463 + 3(498) = 4731 kJ. Formed: 4(804) + 6(463) = 5994 kJ. ΔH = 4731 − 5994 = −1263 kJ mol−1 [3]. (ΔH = bonds broken − bonds formed; count bonds, not atoms.)

  - bonds formed total
  - negative final value with kJ mol−1

  - Wrong subtraction order loses the final mark.
  - A later part allows the (b)(ii) value without penalty (ECF).

- *disguises*:  - Different fuels (alcohols, alkanes)

## [ ] R1.3-09 · Spirit-burner calorimetry: heat loss hides in ΔT
Priority 5 · R1.3.3 · No % for the MCQ. Reports repeatedly flag weak experimental preparation and unit errors in calorimetry.

**Step by step** (`19N.1.HL.TZ0.15`, 1 marks)

A spirit burner of ethanol heats a beaker of water from below, with a thermometer in the water. [Figure: burner with flame directly under the beaker; thermometer standing in the water.] Which quantity is likely to be the least accurate because of this set-up's sources of error?
A. Mass of ethanol burnt
B. Molecular mass of ethanol
C. Mass of water
D. Temperature change

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 5 s): MCQ: one mark for the right reading.
   - *command term*: none
   - *demands*: Choose the reading most affected by the errors.
   - *score mechanics*: 1 mark, all or nothing.
   - *avoid*: Calculating ΔHc to decide.
   - *why*: No calculation is needed. It's a judgement about where the systematic error sits.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 10 s): AO3: judge which measurement the errors affect.
   - *ao level*: AO3
   - *syllabus hook*: R1.3.3: energy released per unit mass; tendency for incomplete combustion (guidance).
   - *textbook gap*: Books describe the method. Exams ask which reading carries the systematic error and in which direction.
   - *avoid*: Thinking every measurement is equally uncertain.
   - *why*: The guidance on energy per unit mass and incomplete combustion is tested through this classic practical. Examiners report weak experimental preparation.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 10 s): Soot or a low ΔHc result: same heat-loss logic.
   - *disguise*: Soot on the beaker, 'comment on the low value', or an improvement question.
   - *heuristic*: Follow the heat. Anything that stops it reaching the water lowers ΔT.
   - *avoid*: Blaming random error for a result that is always too low.
   - *why*: Soot means incomplete combustion, so less energy is released (16N.3.SL.TZ0.2). Any energy not reaching the water shrinks ΔT and the calculated |ΔHc|.
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 5 s): B and C are not affected by heat loss.
   - *definitely wrong*: B: Mr is calculated, not measured: no experimental error.; C: Balance reading of water is precise and not affected by heat loss.
   - *avoid*: Choosing C because water may evaporate slightly: it's a minor effect.
   - *why*: Mr comes from atomic masses, not from the experiment. The water's mass is weighed once and does not change with heat loss.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 5 s): Section 14 values show how low your result is.
   - *sections*: 14
   - *pattern*: Section 14 ΔHc values (e.g. propan-1-ol −2021) as the reference for percentage error.
   - *memorise*: Sources of error and their direction are NOT in the booklet.
   - *avoid*: Expecting the booklet to list sources of error.
   - *why*: Comparing an experimental ΔHc with section 14 shows the size of the heat loss. The experimental magnitude is always smaller.
6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 15 s): Follow the heat: 15 seconds, no numbers.
   - *workflow*: Follow the escaping heat; Which reading shrinks?
   - *fat to trim*: No ΔHc calculation needed.
   - *avoid*: Listing every possible random error.
   - *why*: Heat loss is the dominant error in this set-up. One question, 'which reading needs the heat?', gives the answer.
7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 5 s): D: temperature change is the least accurate.
   - *trigger phrases*: heat loss to surroundings; incomplete combustion / soot
   - *trap boundary*: A ΔT in K equals the ΔT in °C: never add 273 to a difference.
   - *delta*: Right: the systematic heat loss shows in ΔT. Tempting: a mass, measured precisely on a balance.
   - *evidence ref*: 19N.1.HL.TZ0.15
   - *avoid*: Adding 273 to a temperature difference: 20% did (14M.1.SL.TZ2.16); converting 33.5 °C to K (12M.3.SL.TZ1.1).
   - *why*: Heat lost to the surroundings and to the beaker, plus incomplete combustion, means the water warms less. The error collects in ΔT.

**Answer:** D. Heat lost to the air and beaker (and incomplete combustion) means the water warms less than it should, so ΔT is the least accurate. (Heat escapes to air and beaker, so measured ΔT is too small.)
