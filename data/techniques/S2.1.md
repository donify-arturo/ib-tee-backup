## [ ] S2.1-01 · Ionic strength: compare charge first, then ion size
Priority 1 · S2.1.3 · May 2025 examiners called a radius-and-bond-strength question very challenging (25M.1A.SL.TZ1.10). An older lattice enthalpy question had a Difficulty Index of 43% (09N.1.HL.TZ0.17).

**Step by step** (`25N.1A.HL.6219.13`, 1 marks)

Each pair of elements below forms an ionic compound. Which pair gives the compound with the greatest attraction between its ions?
A. Li and O
B. Li and Se
C. K and O
D. K and Se

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 3 s): MCQ stem: one mark, with no credit for working.
   - *command term*: none
   - *demands*: Choose the pair with the greatest electrostatic attraction between its ions.
   - *score mechanics*: One mark, all or nothing.
   - *avoid*: Looking up every radius when the group trend already decides.
   - *why*: Only the letter scores, so a quick ranking is enough and exact values add nothing.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 5 s): Tests lattice strength from ion charge and ion size.
   - *ao level*: AO2
   - *syllabus hook*: S2.1.3: lattice enthalpy measures ionic bond strength, influenced by ion radius and charge.
   - *textbook gap*: Books compare listed lattice enthalpies. Here only element names appear, so you must deduce the ions first.
   - *avoid*: Reasoning from metal reactivity; the question is about the attraction between the ions.
   - *why*: The syllabus names radius and charge as the two factors, so swapping ions tests whether you apply them in order.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 10 s): Elements are named, so deduce the ions before comparing.
   - *disguise*: No radii, charges or lattice enthalpies appear; 'attraction' replaces the phrase lattice enthalpy.
   - *heuristic*: Turn every name into an ion, then ask which pair sits closest.
   - *avoid*: Comparing atoms instead of the ions they form.
   - *why*: Nothing in the stem mentions radius or charge. Writing the ions exposes the usual charge-and-size comparison.
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 10 s): Any option using a larger ion loses to its smaller swap.
   - *definitely wrong*: D: Both ions are the larger of their group pair, so it is the weakest.; B: Swapping Se for the smaller O, same charge, gives stronger attraction.; C: Swapping K for the smaller Li, same charge, gives stronger attraction.
   - *avoid*: Stopping after ruling out D; B and C can also go.
   - *why*: With equal charges, replacing an ion by a smaller one in its group always strengthens attraction, so three options fall without numbers.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 15 s): Section 10 lists ionic radii with their charges.
   - *sections*: 10; 16
   - *pattern*: Section 10 shows Li 76 (1+) and O 140 (2−). Section 16 shows LiCl 864 and KCl 720.
   - *memorise*: Nothing beyond the group trend; radii and charges are printed.
   - *avoid*: Reading atomic radius by mistake: Li is 130 as an atom but 76 as an ion.
   - *why*: Reading radii replaces memorising sizes, and section 16 confirms that a smaller ion gives a larger lattice enthalpy.
6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 20 s): Charges, then sizes: about twenty seconds in total.
   - *workflow*: Find the ion charges; Charges tie? Compare sizes; Smallest pair wins
   - *fat to trim*: Skip configurations and any calculation of attraction.
   - *avoid*: Writing full electron configurations for all four elements.
   - *why*: Once the charges tie, two group comparisons settle it: one for the metals and one for the non-metals.
7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 10 s): Only the pair with both ions smallest earns the mark.
   - *trigger phrases*: equal charges, so radius decides; both ions smallest
   - *trap boundary*: One small ion paired with one large ion is not enough; the mark needs both small.
   - *delta*: B and C each have one small ion. A has two, so its ions sit closest.
   - *evidence ref*: 25N.1A.HL.6219.13
   - *avoid*: Thinking a larger ion gives a stronger lattice, a reported error (09N.1.HL.TZ0.17).
   - *why*: Attraction grows as opposite charges get closer, so the smaller pair wins when the charges are equal.

**Answer:** A. All options combine a 1+ ion with a 2− ion, so size decides. Li⁺ and O²⁻ are the smaller ions, so they attract most strongly. (Charges are equal in every option, so the smallest ions attract most.)

## [ ] S2.1-02 · Moles of an ion: read the formula, and check ions exist
Priority 2 · S2.1.2, S2.1.3 · The related chloride-ion question was among the hardest: 26.81% correct at HL and 28.15% at SL (15M.1.HL.TZ1.3, 15M.1.SL.TZ1.4). Over 70% managed the phosphate version (24N.1.HL.TZ0.1).

**Step by step** (`24N.1.HL.TZ0.1`, 1 marks)

A sample of calcium phosphate, Ca₃(PO₄)₂ (Mr = 310.18), has a mass of 103.39 g. What amount, in mol, of phosphate ions, PO₄³⁻, does it contain?
A. 0.11
B. 0.33
C. 0.67
D. 2.00

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 3 s): MCQ stem: one mark, with no credit for working.
   - *command term*: none
   - *demands*: Give the amount of phosphate ions, not of calcium phosphate.
   - *score mechanics*: One mark, all or nothing.
   - *avoid*: Choosing the first printed number that matches an intermediate step.
   - *why*: Each slip lands on a printed distractor, so the checking must be done before choosing.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 5 s): Tests reading ion counts from an ionic formula.
   - *ao level*: AO2
   - *syllabus hook*: S2.1.3: ionic compounds exist as lattices represented by empirical formulas.
   - *textbook gap*: Books practise moles of a compound. This question asks for moles of one ion inside it.
   - *avoid*: Treating the Mr as the mass of a phosphate ion.
   - *why*: An empirical formula gives the ratio of ions in the lattice, so its subscripts convert moles of compound into moles of ion.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 5 s): Looks like plain moles; really a formula-reading question.
   - *disguise*: A mass and Mr suggest one division, but the requested species is one ion.
   - *heuristic*: Underline what is counted, then ask how many are in one formula.
   - *avoid*: Answering for the compound because the Mr given is the compound's.
   - *why*: The calculation is easy once you see the stem asks about an ion, not the whole compound.
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 10 s): Compare with one-third of a mole of compound by eye.
   - *definitely wrong*: D: Under one mole of compound cannot hold two moles of phosphate.; B: Equals the compound's moles, so it ignores the two phosphates.; A: Below the compound's moles, yet each unit has more than one phosphate.
   - *avoid*: Choosing 2.00 because it matches the subscript.
   - *why*: 103 is about a third of 310, and two phosphates per unit must raise the ion amount above that.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 2 s): No booklet table is needed; everything is in the stem.
   - *sections*: 
   - *pattern*: None needed: the formula itself is the table to read.
   - *memorise*: Phosphate is PO₄³⁻, and a subscript after brackets multiplies the whole ion. Neither is in the booklet.
   - *avoid*: Searching the booklet for phosphate; polyatomic ions are not listed there.
   - *why*: Formula, mass and Mr are all given, so only the meaning of brackets and subscripts has to come from you.
6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 25 s): Two moves: divide, then multiply by the ion count.
   - *workflow*: Mass over Mr; Count ions in formula; Multiply the two
   - *fat to trim*: Skip recalculating Mr and working out calcium's amount.
   - *avoid*: Recalculating the Mr; it is already given.
   - *why*: The mass is close to a simple fraction of the Mr, so little calculation is needed.
7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 15 s): The mark needs moles of compound multiplied by two.
   - *trigger phrases*: moles of compound × 2
   - *trap boundary*: 0.33 is the compound's moles, not phosphate's, so stopping there scores nothing.
   - *delta*: B stops at moles of compound. C multiplies by the two phosphates in each formula.
   - *evidence ref*: 24N.1.HL.TZ0.1
   - *avoid*: Miscounting the polyatomic ions; two sulfates caused the commonest error in 09N.1.SL.TZ0.5.
   - *why*: Each formula unit contains two phosphates, so ion moles are double the compound moles.

**Answer:** C. 103.39 ÷ 310.18 = 0.333 mol of Ca₃(PO₄)₂. Each formula unit has 2 phosphates, so 0.333 × 2 = 0.67 mol. (One formula unit holds two phosphate ions, so double the compound's moles.)

- *ao*: AO2
- *papers*:  - 1A
- *syllabus hook*: S2.1.2: deduce formulas of ionic compounds, including polyatomic ions. S2.1.3: ionic compounds are represented by empirical formulas.
- *textbook gap*: Books teach moles of a compound. Exams ask for moles of one ion inside it, or whether that ion exists at all.

**2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases**
- *trigger phrases*:  - moles of compound × ions per formula unit
  - molecular compound contains no such ions
- *trap boundaries*:  - Stopping at moles of compound gives a listed wrong option (24N.1.HL.TZ0.1).
  - A molecular chloride contains no chloride ions, so it cannot be counted (15M.1.HL.TZ1.3).
- *delta*: Partial thinking finds moles of compound. The full answer also applies the ion count and checks the bonding type.

**3 · Novel application**
- *disguises*:  - A stoichiometry question that is really about formula reading
  - A list of chlorides where one is molecular
  - Polyatomic ion in brackets with a subscript
- *heuristic*: Ask: is it ionic, and how many of this exact ion are in one formula unit?

**4 · Command terms & score mechanics**
- *terms*:  - none (MCQ stem)
- *score mechanics*: One mark, all or nothing. Working earns nothing, and each wrong step usually matches a distractor.

**5 · Time efficiency**
- *workflow*:  - Ionic or molecular?
  - Mass over Mr
  - Times ions per formula
- *fat to trim*: Skip finding Mr yourself when given; skip percentage composition routes.

**6 · Data booklet**
- *sections*:  - —
- *hack*: No booklet table helps here. Polyatomic ion formulas such as phosphate and sulfate are NOT in the booklet and must be memorised.

**7 · Instant elimination**
- *rules*:  - Mass below Mr means under one mole of compound, so cap the ion answer at the subscript.
  - The answer for ions must differ from moles of compound unless the count is one.

**Evidence**
- `24N.1.HL.TZ0.1` (mcq) — Answer C: moles of compound multiplied by two phosphates per formula.
- `24N.1.HL.TZ0.1` (examiner) — Over 70% could find the amount of phosphate ions in a mass of calcium phosphate.
- `15M.1.HL.TZ1.3` (examiner) — Only 26.81% correct; most picked a molecular compound, which contains no chloride ions.
- `15M.1.SL.TZ1.4` (examiner) — Only 28.15% correct; students had to judge ionic versus molecular as well as the quantity.
- `09N.1.SL.TZ0.5` (examiner) — Most common mistake concerned the two sulfates present, giving 4.0 instead of 2.0.

## [ ] S2.1-03 · Structure from data: use melting point and conductivity together
Priority 2 · S2.1.3 · Conductivity by ions rather than electrons is flagged repeatedly (24M.2.SL.TZ1.6, 15M.2.SL.TZ2.3). Only 40% stated a correct physical property in 18N.2.SL.TZ0.4.

**Step by step** (`25M.1A.HL.6219.7`, 1 marks)

A substance melts at 714 °C. It conducts electricity poorly as a solid but well when molten. Which structure is it most likely to have?
A. Simple molecular
B. Ionic lattice
C. Covalent network
D. Metallic lattice

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 3 s): MCQ stem: 'most probable' means best fit to all data.
   - *command term*: none
   - *demands*: Choose the structure that best fits all the given properties.
   - *score mechanics*: One mark, all or nothing.
   - *avoid*: Overthinking unusual substances that the data does not point to.
   - *why*: The wording allows for rare exceptions, so choose the structure fitting everything rather than hunting for special cases.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 5 s): Tests matching raw data to a type of structure.
   - *ao level*: AO2
   - *syllabus hook*: S2.1.3: explain physical properties of ionic compounds, including electrical conductivity and volatility.
   - *textbook gap*: Books describe known compounds' properties. This gives unnamed data and asks you to work backwards to structure.
   - *avoid*: Matching only one reading, then choosing straight away.
   - *why*: Each structure has its own charge carriers and forces, so a few readings are enough to identify it.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 10 s): No compound is named; only the data identifies it.
   - *disguise*: An unnamed substance with three bare numbers or words replaces a familiar compound.
   - *heuristic*: Ask what becomes free on melting that was fixed in the solid.
   - *avoid*: Thinking mobile electrons carry charge in molten ionic compounds (24M.2.SL.TZ1.6).
   - *why*: Without a name you cannot rely on metal-plus-non-metal recall, so the particles have to do the reasoning.
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 10 s): Each wrong structure fails a single reading.
   - *definitely wrong*: D: Metals conduct well as solids; this solid conducts poorly.; A: Simple molecular substances melt low and do not conduct when molten.; C: Covalent networks have no ions to conduct when molten.
   - *avoid*: Keeping covalent network just because the melting point is high.
   - *why*: One mismatch is enough, so each option can go on a single fact, with no calculation at all.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 2 s): Section 16 supports it, but no lookup is needed.
   - *sections*: 16
   - *pattern*: Lattice enthalpies such as NaCl 790 and MgO 3791 kJ mol⁻¹ show strong ionic attraction.
   - *memorise*: Charge carriers per structure are NOT in the booklet: ions, delocalised electrons, or none.
   - *avoid*: Hunting for 714 °C; melting points are not in the section text provided.
   - *why*: Large lattice enthalpies show the strong attraction behind a high melting point, though this question can be answered from the data alone.
6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 15 s): Read two conductivities, then confirm with melting point.
   - *workflow*: Read solid, then molten; Ask what carries charge; Check melting point last
   - *fat to trim*: Skip naming example compounds for each option.
   - *avoid*: Recalling example compounds for each option.
   - *why*: Conductivity removes most options straight away, and melting point is the final check examiners say students skip.
7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 10 s): Only one structure fits all three readings.
   - *trigger phrases*: poor as solid, good when molten; high melting point
   - *trap boundary*: An option fitting only one or two readings earns nothing.
   - *delta*: D fails poor solid conduction. A fails 714 °C. C fails molten conduction.
   - *evidence ref*: 25M.1A.HL.6219.7
   - *avoid*: Using conductivity but ignoring melting point, as examiners reported in 17M.1.HL.TZ1.9.
   - *why*: Ions are locked in the solid and free when molten, and strong attraction explains the high melting point.

**Answer:** B. Ionic lattice: ions are fixed in the solid but mobile when molten, and strong attraction gives the high melting point. (Conducting only when molten, with a high melting point, means ions freed on melting.)

## [ ] S2.1-04 · Ionic lattice: name the force and the structure exactly
Priority 2 · S2.1.2, S2.1.3 · Knowledge of the NaCl structure reached only 52% correct (15N.1.HL.TZ0.9). Written descriptions of ionic bonding are repeatedly flagged as incomplete (19M.2.SL.TZ1.3, 15M.2.HL.TZ2.8, 13M.2.SL.TZ2.5).

**Step by step** (`25N.1A.HL.6207.6`, 1 marks)

A figure shows an expanded view of the sodium chloride lattice, built from Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. Which statement about this lattice is true?
A. Covalent bonds hold the ions in place.
B. The attractions within the lattice are very weak.
C. Every sodium ion has four chloride ions around it.
D. The structure breaks apart when it dissolves in water.

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 3 s): MCQ stem: exactly one statement is correct.
   - *command term*: none
   - *demands*: Choose the one statement that agrees with the ionic lattice model.
   - *score mechanics*: One mark, all or nothing.
   - *avoid*: Hunting for hidden faults in a statement that matches the model.
   - *why*: Only one statement can be right, so finding a clear fault in the others is as useful as proving one.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 5 s): Tests the ionic lattice model through statements.
   - *ao level*: AO1
   - *syllabus hook*: S2.1.3: ionic compounds exist as three-dimensional lattice structures.
   - *textbook gap*: Books label a neat lattice. This asks you to reject wrong claims about force and count.
   - *avoid*: Relying on the figure to count neighbours; an expanded view can mislead.
   - *why*: The ionic bond and lattice arrangement are core knowledge, so each statement checks one part of the model.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 5 s): A figure appears, but the facts alone decide it.
   - *disguise*: A diagram invites counting and visual judgement, when recall of the model is what scores.
   - *heuristic*: Test each statement against particles, force and arrangement, ignoring how the drawing looks.
   - *avoid*: Reading the drawn gaps as evidence of weak attraction.
   - *why*: The picture is expanded, so its gaps can suggest weak forces. Your knowledge of the model matters more than appearance.
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 10 s): Three statements each break one fact of the model.
   - *definitely wrong*: A: Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are held by electrostatic attraction, not covalent bonds.; B: Ionic attractions are strong: section 16 gives 790 kJ mol⁻¹.; C: Each sodium ion in NaCl has six chloride neighbours, not four.
   - *avoid*: Accepting A because polyatomic ions have covalent bonds; Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are single-atom ions.
   - *why*: Ions held by electrostatic attraction exclude covalent bonds and weak forces, and known coordination excludes four.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 10 s): Section 16 shows that NaCl's attraction is not weak.
   - *sections*: 16
   - *pattern*: Alkali metal halide table: Na row, Cl column reads 790 kJ mol⁻¹.
   - *memorise*: NaCl's lattice arrangement and six neighbours per ion are NOT in the booklet.
   - *avoid*: Looking for the neighbour count; it is not in the booklet.
   - *why*: A lattice enthalpy of 790 kJ mol⁻¹ is a large energy, so the printed value contradicts any claim of weak forces.
6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 20 s): Test each statement once, without studying the figure.
   - *workflow*: Say force, then particles; Add 3-D lattice; Test statements against that
   - *fat to trim*: Skip counting ions in the figure and skip naming lattice types.
   - *avoid*: Counting ions in the expanded figure.
   - *why*: Each statement is checked with one fact, so spending time on the figure adds nothing.
7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 15 s): Three statements contradict the model; one survives.
   - *trigger phrases*: ions separate on dissolving
   - *trap boundary*: Four neighbours is wrong for NaCl, even though it sounds specific and confident.
   - *delta*: A, B and C each misstate force or count. D correctly describes the lattice breaking up.
   - *evidence ref*: 25N.1A.HL.6207.6
   - *avoid*: Calling ionic forces weak or covalent; examiners reported covalent or metallic answers for Na₂O (19M.2.SL.TZ1.3).
   - *why*: Force type, force strength and neighbour count are all fixed facts, so any statement breaking one of them is false.

**Answer:** D. Dissolving separates the ions. The others fail: the force is electrostatic attraction, it is strong, and each Na⁺ has six Cl⁻ neighbours. (Strong electrostatic attraction holds the ions; water separates them on dissolving.)
