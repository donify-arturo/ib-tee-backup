## [ ] R1.1-01 · Right number, wrong sign: the sign of ΔH from calorimetry
Priority 1 · R1.1.4, R1.1.2 · 2025 data: 66% and 61% correct on HL and SL versions; 24M version only 39% with 37% on the sign distractor. Flagged in reports from 2010 to 2025.

**Step by step** (`25M.1A.HL.6213.19`, 1 marks)

A spirit burner burns 0.110 mol of fuel beneath a metal calorimeter holding 200 g of water. The water warms from 21.0 °C to 25.0 °C. Use c(water) = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ and Q = mcΔT. Which value is the standard enthalpy change of combustion?
A. −2110 kJ mol⁻¹
B. −30.4 kJ mol⁻¹
C. +30.4 kJ mol⁻¹
D. +2110 kJ mol⁻¹

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 3 s): An MCQ stem: one mark, all or nothing.
   - *command term*: none
   - *demands*: Choose the option with the correct size, unit and sign.
   - *score mechanics*: All or nothing. There is no credit for a correct size with the wrong sign.
   - *avoid*: Spending time on full working: nobody marks it on Paper 1A.
   - *why*: With no working credit, a sign slip costs the same as a total failure. In written papers the same slip costs the final mark.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 75 s): Combustion calorimetry with easy arithmetic and a sign test.
   - *ao level*: AO2
   - *syllabus hook*: R1.1.4: apply Q = mcΔT and ΔH = −Q/n; ΔH units are kJ mol⁻¹.
   - *textbook gap*: Books drill the substitution. This question gives the equation and tests whether you convert a heat into a signed molar enthalpy.
   - *avoid*: Treating it as arithmetic only: 23% chose the right size with the wrong sign (25M.1A.HL.6213.19).
   - *why*: The report links this to R1.1.4 and Tool 1. It discriminated strongly and had the most blanks on the paper, so a routine setup still loses students.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 5 s): The word combustion is your only sign clue here.
   - *disguise*: A labelled apparatus and a list of data make it look like a practical question rather than a sign question.
   - *heuristic*: Ask: did the surroundings warm or cool? Warm means minus; cool means plus.
   - *avoid*: Assuming every ΔH is negative: a dissolving that cools gives a positive ΔH (24N.1.HL.TZ0.18).
   - *why*: No thermometer direction needs interpreting beyond the rise. Examiners vary the process, so you must read the process or temperature change, not the formula.
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 3 s): Combustion is exothermic, so both positive options go.
   - *definitely wrong*: C: A positive ΔH means heat absorbed; combustion releases heat.; D: A positive ΔH means heat absorbed; combustion releases heat.
   - *avoid*: Eliminating on size first, which leaves the sign trap still open.
   - *why*: The sign is fixed by the process before any number is calculated. That leaves one quick size comparison between the two negative options.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 3 s): Q = mcΔT is in section 1; ΔH = −Q/n is not.
   - *sections*: 1
   - *pattern*: Section 1 lists Q = mcΔT. The syllabus says the value of c for water is also supplied.
   - *memorise*: ΔH = −Q/n is NOT in section 1. Memorise it and its kJ mol⁻¹ units.
   - *avoid*: Looking for a molar enthalpy formula in the booklet; section 1 only gives Q = mcΔT.
   - *why*: The stem also prints Q = mcΔT and c, so the booklet adds nothing here. The step from Q to molar ΔH is the part you carry yourself.
6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 30 s): Sign first, then a rough size check.
   - *workflow*: Combustion means minus; Rough Q in kJ; Divide by moles
   - *fat to trim*: Skip exact digits: the two negative options differ far too much to need them.
   - *avoid*: Keying every figure into the calculator before reading the options.
   - *why*: The sign removes two options instantly. Only one rough division then separates the remaining two, because they differ by a factor of about seventy.
7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 10 s): Size 30.4 and a minus sign: only one option has both.
   - *trigger phrases*: −30.4 kJ mol⁻¹; negative by convention for an exothermic change
   - *trap boundary*: +30.4 has correct arithmetic but scores nothing: the MCQ needs the negative sign.
   - *delta*: B and C share the same size. The only difference is the sign that combustion requires.
   - *evidence ref*: 25M.1A.HL.6213.19
   - *avoid*: Stopping at +30.4 because the formula produced a positive number (25M.1A.SL.TZ2.16).
   - *why*: Q from mcΔT is always a positive amount of heat. The sign comes from the direction of transfer, and the convention gives combustion a negative ΔH.

**Answer:** B. Q = 200 × 4.18 × 4.0 = 3344 J = 3.34 kJ. 3.34 ÷ 0.110 = 30.4, and combustion is exothermic, so ΔH = −30.4 kJ mol⁻¹. (Q is only a size; ΔH = −Q/n, so heat released means negative.)

## [ ] R1.1-02 · Solution mass in Q, limiting reactant in n: the full molar enthalpy calculation
Priority 1 · R1.1.4 · Findings list calorimetry calculations as difficult in Nov 2018 and May 2011; 55% correct on the 25M MCQ version.

**Step by step** (`18N.2.HL.TZ0.1`, 2 marks)

Iron powder (3.26 g, M = 55.85 g mol⁻¹) is added to 80.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ copper(II) sulfate solution in a calorimeter: Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s). The solution's temperature rises by at most 7.5 °C. Assume the solution takes up all the heat released and has a density of 1.00 g cm⁻³. Using c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹, calculate ΔH for this reaction in kJ. [2]

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 5 s): Calculate: working shown, final value signed.
   - *command term*: Calculate
   - *demands*: Show Q, then the enthalpy change for the reaction in kJ with its sign.
   - *score mechanics*: Two marks, one per stage: heat from mcΔT, then the signed molar value.
   - *avoid*: Writing only a final number, which risks both marks if it is wrong.
   - *why*: Calculate requires relevant stages. Showing Q separately protects the first mark even if a later mole or sign error appears.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 120 s): Paper 2 calorimetry linking stoichiometry to R1.1.4.
   - *ao level*: AO2
   - *syllabus hook*: R1.1.4: apply Q = mcΔT and ΔH = −Q/n to find a reaction's enthalpy change.
   - *textbook gap*: Book examples usually state the moles. Exams make you find which reactant limits before dividing.
   - *avoid*: Dividing by moles of iron just because iron is the solid you weighed (18N.2.HL.TZ0.1).
   - *why*: The written form separates heat from molar enthalpy, so each stage is tested. The limiting reactant decides which moles belong in the division.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 5 s): An iron displacement hides a standard neutralisation-type method.
   - *disguise*: A solid reactant and two given amounts make the mass and mole choices look ambiguous.
   - *heuristic*: m is whatever heats up; n is whatever runs out.
   - *avoid*: Adding iron's mass to m because it sits in the calorimeter (11M.2.HL.TZ2.1 shows solid-only mass rejected).
   - *why*: Whatever reacts, the method is identical because only the solution is heated and the limiting reactant sets the amount reacting.
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 5 s): A positive answer or an answer using 3.26 g is out.
   - *definitely wrong*: a positive ΔH: The temperature rose, so heat was released.; Q using 3.26 g as the mass: The solution is what absorbs the heat.; dividing by 0.0584 mol: Iron is in excess, so not all of it reacts.
   - *avoid*: Accepting 2.5 kJ as final: it is a heat for this sample, not the reaction's ΔH.
   - *why*: The temperature rose, so the sign is fixed. The solid's mass is not what warms in this model, so any Q built on it fails.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 10 s): Sections 1 and 2 give the tools; ΔH = −Q/n is memorised.
   - *sections*: 1
   - *pattern*: Section 1: Q = mcΔT, n = m/M, n = CV. The question directs you to section 2 as well for c.
   - *memorise*: ΔH = −Q/n and water's density, 1.00 g cm⁻³, are NOT in section 1.
   - *avoid*: Hunting for the density of water: treat it as 1.00 g cm⁻³ from memory.
   - *why*: The question itself points to sections 1 and 2. Section 1 supplies Q = mcΔT, n = m/M and n = CV, which carry the whole method.
6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 20 s): Moles once, Q once, divide, sign.
   - *workflow*: Mass means solution, not solid; Moles mean limiting reactant; Rise means minus sign
   - *fat to trim*: Do not find the copper mass or percentage yield; this part needs neither.
   - *avoid*: Converting ΔT to kelvin: a change of 7.5 °C is already a change of 7.5 K.
   - *why*: A short, fixed order avoids rereading the stem and catches the three reported errors: wrong mass, no division and missing sign.
7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 15 s): One mark for Q, one for signed ΔH per limiting moles.
   - *trigger phrases*: 80.0 g × 4.18 × 7.5 gives 2.5 kJ; −2.5 ÷ 0.0160 gives −1.6 × 10² kJ
   - *trap boundary*: No second mark without the division by moles and the negative sign.
   - *delta*: Q alone earns one mark. Full credit needs the division by 0.0160 mol and the minus sign.
   - *evidence ref*: 18N.2.HL.TZ0.1
   - *avoid*: Writing ΔH = mcΔT; a heat is not a molar enthalpy (05M.2.SL.TZ0.1).
   - *why*: Marks split at Q versus ΔH. Examiners note some students stop at q, while others forget the negative sign and lose the second mark only.

**Answer:** Q = 80.0 × 4.18 × 7.5 = 2.5 × 10³ J = 2.5 kJ. CuSO₄ limits (0.0160 mol against 0.0584 mol Fe). ΔH = −2.5 ÷ 0.0160 = −1.6 × 10² kJ for the reaction as written. (Q uses solution mass; divide by moles of the limiting reactant; exothermic means negative.)

## [ ] R1.1-03 · Never add 273 to a temperature change
Priority 2 · R1.1.1, R1.1.4 · Hardest recorded item for the sub-topic is this trap: 05M 30% correct. Still 68% at SL and 78% at HL in 2014.

**Step by step** (`14M.1.HL.TZ2.19`, 1 marks)

A 100 g sample of water receives 2100 J of energy. Water's specific heat capacity is 4.2 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. By how much does its temperature rise?
A. 5 °C
B. 278 K
C. 0.2 °C
D. 20 °C

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 2 s): An MCQ stem with no working credited.
   - *command term*: none
   - *demands*: Select the correct temperature rise.
   - *score mechanics*: All or nothing: one mark for the correct option.
   - *avoid*: Writing unit conversions in the margin; there is nothing to convert.
   - *why*: Only the option counts. Knowing that a 5 °C change equals a 5 K change lets you reject the distractor at once.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 30 s): A one-step rearrangement that tests the idea of a temperature change.
   - *ao level*: AO2
   - *syllabus hook*: R1.1.1: understand the difference between heat and temperature; R1.1.4 uses Q = mcΔT.
   - *textbook gap*: Books teach that kelvin is required for gas laws. Students then wrongly apply this conversion to a temperature change.
   - *avoid*: Adding 273 to get 278 K: nearly 14% at HL and nearly 20% at SL did (14M.1.HL.TZ2.19, 14M.1.SL.TZ2.16).
   - *why*: The report says this item was designed to test understanding of a temperature difference, not arithmetic. A kelvin distractor exists purely to catch this idea.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 3 s): The kelvin option looks technically careful.
   - *disguise*: Two options differ only in a 273 shift, while c is quoted per kelvin.
   - *heuristic*: If you see ΔT, ask whether 278 degrees of heating is even plausible.
   - *avoid*: Picking the only option in SI units because it looks more formal.
   - *why*: Mixed units in the options suggest a conversion is expected. The arithmetic gives 5 and needs no conversion at all.
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 2 s): A rise of 278 K would boil water many times over.
   - *definitely wrong*: B: A rise of 278 K is implausible and comes from adding 273 to ΔT.
   - *avoid*: Eliminating A because it is not in kelvin.
   - *why*: A rise that large is physically absurd for a small energy. It also carries the signature of the reported 273 error.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 3 s): Q = mcΔT in section 1; the K conversion is section 4.
   - *sections*: 1; 4
   - *pattern*: Section 1: Q = mcΔT. Section 4: temperature in K = °C + 273.15, for readings only.
   - *memorise*: That a change in °C equals the same change in K is NOT stated in the booklet.
   - *avoid*: Applying the section 4 line to ΔT because it is the only temperature conversion listed.
   - *why*: Section 4's conversion applies to an actual temperature reading. It tells you how to convert a reading, not a difference between two readings.
6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 10 s): Divide mentally: 2100 over 420.
   - *workflow*: Multiply m by c; Divide energy by that; Keep the degree unchanged
   - *fat to trim*: Do not convert any unit or check the options with extra calculations.
   - *avoid*: Doing a full rearrangement with a calculator for simple numbers.
   - *why*: The numbers are chosen to divide cleanly, so a calculator step is not needed. That leaves spare time for harder items on the paper.
7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 5 s): The value 5 in °C is correct; 278 K is not.
   - *trigger phrases*: ΔT = 5 °C; same size in K and °C
   - *trap boundary*: Adding 273 to the change gives no credit in MCQs and costs marks in written calculations.
   - *delta*: Tempting B and correct A come from identical arithmetic. B then wrongly adds 273.
   - *evidence ref*: 14M.1.HL.TZ2.19
   - *avoid*: Thinking a kelvin unit makes an answer more rigorous; 278 K is a reading, not a rise.
   - *why*: One degree Celsius and one kelvin are the same step size, so a difference is unchanged. Only absolute readings shift by 273.

**Answer:** A. ΔT = 2100 ÷ (100 × 4.2) = 5, and a 5 °C rise is the same as a 5 K rise. (A temperature change has the same size in °C and K.)

## [ ] R1.1-07 · The exothermic pair: temperature up, enthalpy down
Priority 2 · R1.1.2, R1.1.3 · 2025 data: just over 50% (6219.20) and 70–76% on profile versions. A persistent half-right distractor pattern.

**Step by step** (`25M.1A.HL.6219.20`, 1 marks)

Pick the correct description of an exothermic reaction.
A. The temperature falls and products have more enthalpy than reactants.
B. The temperature falls and products have less enthalpy than reactants.
C. The temperature rises and products have more enthalpy than reactants.
D. The temperature rises and products have less enthalpy than reactants.

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 2 s): An MCQ stem with paired claims.
   - *command term*: none
   - *demands*: Select the option where both claims are correct.
   - *score mechanics*: All or nothing: a half-right pair scores zero.
   - *avoid*: Deciding on the first half only and guessing the second.
   - *why*: A paired option needs one reasoning chain, not two facts. Getting the direction of flow right gives both halves together.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 20 s): A 2025 two-part statement testing system versus surroundings.
   - *ao level*: AO2
   - *syllabus hook*: R1.1.2: temperature increases with exothermic and decreases with endothermic reactions. R1.1.3 links stability.
   - *textbook gap*: Books define each term separately. Exams join temperature and stability in one option to test both.
   - *avoid*: Thinking energy lost by the system means a cooler mixture; a significant number said the temperature fell (25M.1A.HL.6219.20).
   - *why*: Each option pairs a temperature claim with an enthalpy claim. Examiners built half-right distractors, so knowing only one part leaves a coin toss.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 3 s): No diagram: just words about a familiar definition.
   - *disguise*: Plain sentences that look like recall but actually test two linked ideas.
   - *heuristic*: Picture a thermometer in the surroundings, then an arrow going down in energy.
   - *avoid*: Reading 'releases energy' as the mixture getting colder.
   - *why*: Removing the graph removes your visual anchor, so you must reason from energy flow. The same pair appears in tables, profiles and bond statements.
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 3 s): Any falling temperature or higher products is out.
   - *definitely wrong*: A: Both parts describe an endothermic change.; B: An exothermic change warms the surroundings.; C: Products lose energy in an exothermic change.
   - *avoid*: Keeping C because the temperature half is right.
   - *why*: Temperature fall contradicts energy entering the surroundings. Higher-enthalpy products contradict energy leaving the system. Together those remove three options at once.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 2 s): Nothing useful in the booklet; this is reasoning.
   - *sections*: 
   - *pattern*: No table applies; reason from energy flow.
   - *memorise*: NOT in the booklet: exo means energy to surroundings, temperature up, products lower and more stable.

  - With a small percentage on ΔT, a ± close to or above ΔH is wrong. This check fails if ΔT itself is very small, so its percentage uncertainty is large.

## [ ] R1.1-08 · Read ΔH from the plateaus, not the peak

**Step by step** (`14M.1.HL.TZ2.14`, 1 marks)

An enthalpy profile for an exothermic reaction has three heights marked, x highest, then y, then z lowest. The reactants sit at y. The curve climbs to a peak at x, then falls to the products at z. What is ΔH?
A. y − z
B. z − y
C. x − z
D. z − x

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 2 s): An MCQ stem; a single expression wins.
   - *command term*: none
   - *demands*: Select the expression equal to ΔH.
   - *score mechanics*: All or nothing: one mark for one expression.

   - *why*: The options test sign and which height matters. Knowing the definition settles both without any further steps.

5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 2 s): No section helps; this is a definition.
   - *sections*: 
   - *pattern*: No table applies; read levels from the diagram.

6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 10 s): Ignore the peak, then write end minus start.

7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 5 s): Products minus reactants gives z − y: option B.

## [ ] R1.1-12 · Deduce exothermic or endothermic from what heating does

Hydrated cobalt(II) ions and chloride ions are at equilibrium: [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺(aq, pink) + 4Cl⁻(aq) ⇌ [CoCl₄]²⁻(aq, blue) + 6H₂O(l). A pink equilibrium mixture turns purplish-blue when warmed. Deduce, with a reason, whether forming [CoCl₄]²⁻ is exothermic or endothermic. [1]
