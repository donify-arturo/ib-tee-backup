## [ ] R2.1-01 · Limiting reactant: divide moles by coefficient
Priority 1 · R2.1.3, R2.1.4 · 73% correct at HL and 66% at SL; examiners call the SL version highly discriminating. Limiting reactant is the top error phrase in the reports.

**Step by step** (`25M.1A.HL.6213.23`, 1 marks)

Magnesium (0.20 mol) is mixed with hydrochloric acid containing 0.20 mol of HCl.
Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g)
Which pair gives the limiting reactant and the theoretical yield of H2 in mol?
A. Mg, 0.10
B. Mg, 0.20
C. HCl, 0.05
D. HCl, 0.10

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 5 s): No command term: one mark for the full pair.
   - *command term*: none
   - *demands*: Pick the one option with the correct limiting reactant and correct yield.
   - *score mechanics*: All or nothing: one mark for the correct pair.
   - *avoid*: Choosing the first option that has your first result.
   - *why*: Each distractor is built from one correct step, so half-finished thinking still finds an option.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 10 s): Limiting reactant sets the yield; this asks for both.
   - *ao level*: AO2
   - *syllabus hook*: R2.1.3: the limiting reactant determines the theoretical yield.
   - *textbook gap*: Books practise the two steps apart; this tests them as one linked pair.
   - *avoid*: Stopping once the limiting reactant is named; weaker students did this (25M.1A.HL.6213.23).
   - *why*: Product stops when one reactant runs out, so only that reactant's moles can set the yield.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 10 s): Equal moles look balanced, but the coefficients are not.
   - *disguise*: Both reactants have the same number of moles, which hides the limiting reactant.
   - *heuristic*: Ask: if all of the Mg reacted, would there be enough HCl?
   - *avoid*: Assuming neither limits because both amounts are 0.20 mol.
   - *why*: Moles are only comparable after dividing by coefficients, as the equation sets the rate of use.
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 10 s): Options A and B need more HCl than exists.
   - *definitely wrong*: A: Mg cannot limit: it would need twice the HCl present.; B: Mg cannot limit, for the same reason.; C: 0.20 mol HCl gives half as much H2, not a quarter.
   - *avoid*: Keeping A because 0.10 looks right.
   - *why*: If Mg limited, all 0.20 mol would need 0.40 mol HCl, and only 0.20 mol is present.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 5 s): No booklet needed: the amounts are already in moles.
   - *sections*: 
   - *pattern*: None here: the equation's coefficients do all the work.
   - *memorise*: Coefficients are read from the balanced equation; no booklet table supplies them.
   - *avoid*: Looking up Mg and Cl values you will not use.
   - *why*: Ar values only matter when masses must become moles. This ratio comes from the equation.
6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 30 s): Two divisions and one ratio, about 30 seconds.
   - *workflow*: Divide moles by coefficient; Smallest one limits; Halve HCl for H2
   - *fat to trim*: No masses or molar masses are needed; everything is already in moles.
   - *avoid*: Calculating a yield from both reactants.
   - *why*: The second division settles the limiting reactant, and its value is already the H2 amount here.
7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 20 s): HCl limits, and 2 HCl gives 1 H2.
   - *trigger phrases*: limiting reactant HCl; 0.10 mol H2
   - *trap boundary*: The mark needs the correct reactant and correct yield together.
   - *delta*: A has the yield but names Mg. C names HCl but halves the ratio twice. Only D has both.
   - *evidence ref*: 25M.1A.HL.6213.23
   - *avoid*: Option A: right yield, wrong limiting reactant, the top distractor (25M.1A.SL.TZ2.18).
   - *why*: The key needs both halves. HCl has a coefficient of 2, so 0.20 mol is used twice as fast as Mg.

**Answer:** D. HCl is limiting (0.20 ÷ 2 = 0.10, below Mg's 0.20 ÷ 1), giving 0.10 mol H2. (Smallest moles ÷ coefficient limits; its ratio then sets the product.)

## [ ] R2.1-02 · Limiting reactant with mixed units
Priority 2 · R2.1.2, R2.1.3, R2.1.4 · Less than 60% at HL and about half at SL; examiners noted concern about the mental arithmetic needed.

**Step by step** (`24N.1.HL.TZ0.3`, 1 marks)

A sample of 4.60 g of sodium reacts with 1.14 dm3 of chlorine gas at STP, where the molar volume is 22.7 dm3 mol-1.
2Na(s) + Cl2(g) → 2NaCl(s)
What mass of sodium chloride, in g, can form?
A. 1.17
B. 2.92
C. 5.84
D. 11.7

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 5 s): No command term: one mark, four numeric options.
   - *command term*: none
   - *demands*: Select the maximum mass of product from the given amounts.
   - *score mechanics*: All or nothing: one mark for the correct mass.
   - *avoid*: Trusting an answer only because it appears in the list.
   - *why*: Options come from common slips, so a slip usually lands on a printed value. Matching is not proof.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 10 s): Two units, one limiting reactant, and the answer in grams.
   - *ao level*: AO2
   - *syllabus hook*: R2.1.3: identify limiting and excess reactants; R2.1.2: reacting masses and volumes.
   - *textbook gap*: Books often show one unit per example; this mixes grams and dm3 in one line.
   - *avoid*: Comparing 4.60 with 1.14 directly.
   - *why*: Masses and volumes cannot be compared directly. Only moles count particles, and the equation works in particles.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 10 s): The gas volume is just moles in disguise.
   - *disguise*: A solid mass and a gas volume look like different kinds of problem.
   - *heuristic*: Ask: what would each number be in moles?
   - *avoid*: Using 22.7 at other conditions, which lost marks (24M.2.SL.TZ1.1).
   - *why*: At STP each mole occupies 22.7 dm3, so dividing by it gives moles, like dividing mass by molar mass.
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 10 s): 11.7 g is more than all the reactants combined.
   - *definitely wrong*: D: It exceeds the combined mass of the reactants.; B: It treats each Cl2 as giving one NaCl, not two.
   - *avoid*: Picking D because it uses the full sodium mass.
   - *why*: Mass is conserved. About 3.5 g of chlorine plus 4.60 g of sodium gives about 8 g at most.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 10 s): Molar volume is printed; take Na and Cl values from Ar.
   - *sections*: 
   - *pattern*: Na 22.99 plus Cl 35.45 gives NaCl 58.44, using the booklet's Ar values as the syllabus directs.
   - *memorise*: Moles from gas volume is volume ÷ molar volume. Booklet section text was not supplied, so no number is cited.
   - *avoid*: Using rounded Ar values in a written calculation.
   - *why*: The syllabus says to use booklet Ar values to two decimals, which gives the molar mass 58.44 for NaCl.
6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 60 s): Round the moles and finish in about 60 seconds.
   - *workflow*: Round to friendly moles; Cl2 limits, double it; Times 58.44 for mass
   - *fat to trim*: Skip the excess sodium remaining; the question does not ask for it.
   - *avoid*: Long division that uses up time; examiners noted the arithmetic load (24N.1.HL.TZ0.3).
   - *why*: The options differ by factors of about two, so rounded moles are enough to separate them.
7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 30 s): Cl2 limits: about 0.05 mol gives about 0.10 mol NaCl.
   - *trigger phrases*: limiting reagent; maximum yield
   - *trap boundary*: Using Na as limiting gives option D, which assumes more chlorine than exists.
   - *delta*: D uses all the sodium. C uses all the chlorine, the true limit, with two NaCl per Cl2.
   - *evidence ref*: 24N.1.HL.TZ0.3
   - *avoid*: Skipping the limiting check; under 60% found the yield (24N.1.HL.TZ0.3).
   - *why*: Na gives 0.20 ÷ 2 = 0.10, and Cl2 gives about 0.05 ÷ 1. The smaller value decides the product.

**Answer:** C. Na is 0.20 mol and Cl2 about 0.05 mol, so Cl2 limits. About 0.10 mol NaCl × 58.44 is about 5.84 g; exact Cl2 moles give 5.87 g, closest to C. (Convert mass and volume to moles, then compare by coefficients.)

## [ ] R2.1-09 · Limiting reactant from a graph
Priority 2 · R2.1.3 · Flagged in May 2025 reports: using graphical data to justify which reactant is limiting.

**Step by step** (`25M.1B.HL.6214.3`, 1 marks)

The same mass of solid NaHCO3 reacts with a constant volume of HCl(aq) at three concentrations. A data logger records the mass of the mixture every five seconds.
The graph shows mass, starting about 9 g, against time for 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5 mol dm-3 acid. The curves level off at different final masses: highest for 0.5 mol dm-3, lower for 1.0 mol dm-3.
Suggest, with a reason, which reactant was limiting in the 0.5 and 1.0 mol dm-3 reactions.

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 5 s): Suggest with a reason: name it, then justify.
   - *command term*: Suggest
   - *demands*: Name the limiting reagent and support it using the graph.
   - *score mechanics*: One mark, needing the reagent and the reason together.
   - *avoid*: Leaving the reason line vague.
   - *why*: Suggest asks for a proposal, and 'with a reason' makes the justification part of the single mark.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 15 s): Graph data, not moles, identifies the limiting reactant here.
   - *ao level*: AO3
   - *syllabus hook*: R2.1.3: identify limiting and excess reactants from given data.
   - *textbook gap*: Books use mole calculations; here the proof comes from the graph's plateaus.
   - *avoid*: Assuming concentrated acid can never be limiting.
   - *why*: Mass falls as CO2 escapes, so the mass lost measures product formed and therefore shows what limited it.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 10 s): A rate experiment secretly asks about stoichiometry.
   - *disguise*: A mass-time rate graph looks like a kinetics question.
   - *heuristic*: Ask: does this part depend on slope or plateau?
   - *avoid*: Using steepness as your reason.
   - *why*: Slope shows speed and plateau shows total amount, so one graph tests both ideas.
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 10 s): NaHCO3 as limiting would give the same final mass.
   - *definitely wrong*: NaHCO3 as the limiting reagent: Equal solid would give equal mass loss, and the plateaus differ.; A reason based on speed or slope: Slope shows rate, not which reactant ran out.
   - *avoid*: Answering from concentration alone.
   - *why*: If the solid ran out, each run would make the same CO2 from equal solid, so plateaus would match.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 3 s): No booklet data needed: the graph holds the evidence.
   - *sections*: 
   - *pattern*: None: read the plateaus.
   - *memorise*: Mass loss in an open container measures gas escaping, here CO2.
   - *avoid*: Looking up molar masses.
   - *why*: This is a comparison of measured totals, so no constants or atomic masses are used.
6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 30 s): Read the plateaus and write one clear sentence.
   - *workflow*: Compare plateau heights; Changed reactant limits; State reagent AND reason
   - *fat to trim*: Skip gradients and mole calculations on this part.
   - *avoid*: Calculating moles of NaHCO3.
   - *why*: Only the final levels matter, so the rest of each curve needs no reading.
7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 25 s): HCl limits: same solid mass but different CO2 mass.
   - *trigger phrases*: same initial mass of solid; different mass of CO2; final mass same if NaHCO3 limiting
   - *trap boundary*: The single mark needs reagent AND reason; a converse argument is accepted.
   - *delta*: HCl with no reason scores nothing. HCl plus different CO2 or final mass scores the mark.
   - *evidence ref*: 25M.1B.HL.6214.3
   - *avoid*: Giving HCl without the evidence.
   - *why*: Fixed solid with changing totals means the solid did not control the total, so the acid did.

**Answer:** HCl is limiting. The same mass of solid gave different masses of CO2 and different final masses; if NaHCO3 were limiting, final masses would match. (Final mass depends on the limiting reactant, so differing totals expose it.)

## [ ] R2.1-05 · Balancing combustion: count the oxygen in the fuel
Priority 3 · R2.1.1 · 77% correct, but balanced equations are flagged as difficult across many sessions and cost marks in many options questions.

**Step by step** (`25M.1A.HL.6213.20`, 1 marks)

Ethanol, C2H5OH, burns completely in oxygen to give carbon dioxide and water.
When balanced with the smallest whole numbers, what is the coefficient of O2?
A. 1
B. 2
C. 3
D. 7

1. **4 · Command terms & score mechanics** (about 5 s): No command term: one mark for one coefficient.
   - *command term*: none
   - *demands*: Balance the equation and report the O2 coefficient only.
   - *score mechanics*: All or nothing: one mark.
   - *avoid*: Answering with the number of oxygen atoms.
   - *why*: Coefficient questions test the whole balance without a written equation, so one slip means no mark.
2. **1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge** (about 5 s): Combustion equation with an oxygen hidden in the fuel.
   - *ao level*: AO2
   - *syllabus hook*: R2.1.1: deduce equations when reactants and products are specified.
   - *textbook gap*: Books mainly balance hydrocarbons; ethanol adds one oxygen to the left side.
   - *avoid*: Treating ethanol like a hydrocarbon.
   - *why*: Atoms are conserved, so every oxygen source on the left counts, including the fuel's.
3. **3 · Novel application** (about 5 s): A familiar combustion, with a fuel that brings its own oxygen.
   - *disguise*: It looks like ordinary hydrocarbon balancing.
   - *heuristic*: Ask: does the fuel's formula contain oxygen?
   - *avoid*: Reusing a memorised hydrocarbon pattern.
   - *why*: Any oxygen-containing fuel works the same way, so the subtraction applies to all alcohols.
4. **7 · Instant elimination** (about 10 s): A and B leave far too little oxygen on the left.
   - *definitely wrong*: A: Provides 3 O atoms; products need more.; B: Provides 5 O atoms; products need more.; D: It treats the 7 product oxygens as the O2 coefficient.
   - *avoid*: Picking 7 because it is the product oxygen count.
   - *why*: Products need 7 O atoms. A provides 3 and B provides 5, so oxygen does not balance.
5. **6 · Data booklet** (about 3 s): No booklet help: know the combustion products.
   - *sections*: 
   - *pattern*: None: count atoms from the formulas.
   - *memorise*: Complete combustion of carbon compounds gives CO2 and H2O.
   - *avoid*: Searching for an equation table.
   - *why*: Complete combustion products are chemical knowledge. Atom counting needs no data.
6. **5 · Time efficiency** (about 25 s): Atom count only, about 25 seconds.
   - *workflow*: Balance C, then H; Count O on right; Subtract one, then halve
   - *fat to trim*: No state symbols or masses are needed for this mark.
   - *avoid*: Balancing by trial and error.
   - *why*: Carbon and hydrogen fix the products immediately, so oxygen is the last and only sum.
7. **2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases** (about 25 s): C2H5OH + 3O2 → 2CO2 + 3H2O.
   - *trigger phrases*: smallest whole numbers; complete combustion
   - *trap boundary*: The coefficient is for the O2 molecule, not for oxygen atoms.
   - *delta*: D counts seven product oxygens and stops. C subtracts the fuel's oxygen and halves.
   - *evidence ref*: 25M.1A.HL.6213.20
   - *avoid*: Ignoring ethanol's oxygen, the commonest error, leading to D (25M.1A.SL.TZ2.17).
   - *why*: Products hold 4 + 3 = 7 O atoms. Ethanol gives 1, so O2 supplies 6, which is 3 O2.

**Answer:** C. C2H5OH + 3O2 → 2CO2 + 3H2O; the coefficient of O2 is 3. (Oxygen needed from O2 equals product oxygen minus the fuel's oxygen.)

## [ ] R2.1-10 · Titration data: endpoint, then concentration
Priority 3 · R2.1.2 · Examiners report many could not draw best-fit lines and were imprecise reading the volume; concentration was then mostly correct.

**Step by step** (`15M.2.HL.TZ2.1`, 4 marks)

Hydrochloric acid was added in 5.0 cm3 portions to 25.0 cm3 of 1.00 mol dm-3 NaOH, up to 50.0 cm3 of acid, and the temperature was recorded. Both solutions started at the same temperature.
The graph has these approximate points (volume in cm3, temperature in °C): 0, 25.0; 5, 25.8; 10, 27.0; 15, 28.0; 20, 28.8; 25, 29.8; 30, 30.1; 35, 30.0; 40, 30.0; 45, 30.0; 50, 29.8.
