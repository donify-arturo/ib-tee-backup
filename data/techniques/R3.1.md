## [ ] R3.1-T01 · pH vs pOH: −log of a base concentration is pOH, not pH
Priority 1 · R3.1.4, R3.1.5, R3.1.9 · 45% correct (25M.1A.HL.6213.32)

**1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge**
- *ao*: AO2
- *papers*:  - 1A
  - 2
- *syllabus hook*: pH = −log[H+] and pOH = −log[OH−] (data booklet section 1); Kw = 1.00 × 10−14 at 298 K (section 2). pH + pOH = 14.00 follows from these but is not printed in the data booklet: memorise it.
- *textbook gap*: Books teach the formulas separately; exams hide the base inside an answer option so the reflex −log(c) is applied to [OH−].

**2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases**
- *trigger phrases*:  - [OH−] =
  - pOH =
  - pH = 14.00 − pOH
- *trap boundaries*:  - A weak-base pH that comes out below 7 gets no error-carried-forward credit (25M.2.HL.6221.3).
  - Stopping at pOH when pH was asked is a recurring lost mark (24M.2.SL.TZ2.3).
- *delta*: Full marks need the conversion step written out: [OH−] → pOH → pH (or Kw/[OH−] → [H+] → pH).

**3 · Novel application**
- *disguises*:  - 'Which solution has a pH of 9.0?' with base concentrations as options
  - [OH−] asked for an acidic solution (pH 4.50)
- *heuristic*: Before any arithmetic, decide which side of 7 the answer must be on and which ion the concentration describes.

**4 · Command terms & score mechanics**
- *terms*:  - Calculate
  - Determine
- *score mechanics*: Calculate = working shown; a bare final value only scores if the markscheme says 'Award [n] for correct final answer'.

**5 · Time efficiency**
- *workflow*:  - Base? → find pOH first
  - pH = 14 − pOH
  - Sanity: base → pH > 7, acid → pH < 7
- *fat to trim*: Once pH + pOH = 14.00 is memorised, you don't need to rebuild it from Kw each time.

**6 · Data booklet**
- *sections*:  - 1
  - 2
- *hack*: Section 1 prints pH, pOH and Kw = [H+][OH−]; section 2 gives Kw's value. The shortcut pH + pOH = 14.00 is not printed: derive it from those two sections or memorise it.

**7 · Instant elimination**
- *rules*:  - A strong base at 10^−n mol dm−3 (n ≤ 6) has pH = 14 − n; any option giving pH = n is the pOH trap.
  - At pH 4.50, [OH−] must be below 10^−7: eliminate 10^−4 and 10^−5 options instantly.
  - A base concentration of 10^−9 mol dm−3 cannot give pH 9: water's own ions dominate, so pH ≈ 7.

**Evidence**
- `25M.1A.HL.6213.32` (mcq) — 45% correct; 24% chose the option that treats −log[base] as pH
- `25M.1A.SL.TZ2.25` (examiner) — 31% chose the pOH-for-pH distractor
- `25N.1A.HL.6219.31` (question) — [OH−] from pH 4.50
- `24M.2.SL.TZ2.3` (examiner) — many stopped at pOH
- `25M.2.HL.6221.3` (markscheme) — no ECF if pH < 7 for a base

## [ ] R3.1-T10 · Reading and sketching pH curves: start pH, equivalence volume and pH, half-equivalence
Priority 1 · R3.1.8, R3.1.13, R3.1.17 · 42% correct (24N.1.HL.TZ0.27)

**1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge**
- *ao*: AO2–AO3
- *papers*:  - 1A
  - 1B
  - 2
- *syllabus hook*: Curves of strong/weak combinations have characteristic shapes; at half-equivalence pH = pKa.
- *textbook gap*: Textbooks show acid-in-flask curves; exams flip it (acid added to base, base added to acid) and change the axis label.

**2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases**
- *trigger phrases*:  - equivalence point
  - half-equivalence: pH = pKa
  - buffer region
  - pH at equivalence > 7 / < 7
- *trap boundaries*:  - Sketches without a vertical section, or with the wrong equivalence location, lose marks (19M.2.HL.TZ1.5, 18N.2.HL.TZ0.6).
  - Changing the base's strength does not move the equivalence volume (24M.2.HL.TZ1.5).
  - Using the final burette volume instead of the volume at equivalence (24N.2.SL.TZ0.4); titre = final − initial reading (25N.1B.HL.6208.1).
- *delta*: Equivalence volume comes from moles; equivalence pH from the salt; buffer region and pKa from half-equivalence.

**3 · Novel application**
- *disguises*:  - Weak base titrated with HCl: find pKb from the curve
  - Four sketches where the x-axis says 'volume of acid added' vs 'volume of alkali added'
  - Excess ammonia added to HCl
- *heuristic*: Read the axis label first; then the start pH tells you what is in the flask, the jump tells you equivalence.

**4 · Command terms & score mechanics**
- *terms*:  - Sketch
  - Determine
  - Deduce
- *score mechanics*: Sketch: shape features each earn a mark: start pH, jump position, equivalence pH, end plateau.

**5 · Time efficiency**
- *workflow*:  - Axis label → what is added
  - Start pH → strong or weak in flask
  - Half of equivalence volume → read pH
  - Weak acid: pKa = pH there; weak base: pKb = 14 − pH there
- *fat to trim*: No calculation needed to find pKa or pKb from a curve: it is a reading.

**6 · Data booklet**
- *sections*:  - 1
  - 2
- *hack*: pKa + pKb = 14.00 is not printed in the booklet; it follows from Kw (section 2). Memorise it so a half-equivalence reading for a base converts in one step.

**7 · Instant elimination**
- *rules*:  - For a base titrated with acid, the pH at half-equivalence is 14 − pKb: if pH there is 9, the option 'pKb = 9' is the trap; the answer is 5.
  - A strong base titrated with a strong acid starts near pH 13–14 and ends low: eliminate curves that start low.
  - Weak base + strong acid → equivalence pH below 7: eliminate curves with the jump centred at pH ≥ 7.

**Evidence**
- `24N.1.HL.TZ0.27` (mcq) — 42% correct; pKb of a weak base from a curve (14 − 9 = 5)
- `25M.1A.HL.6207.3` (mcq) — strong base with strong acid: axis-label trap
- `24M.2.HL.TZ1.5` (examiner) — shifted equivalence when only the strength changed
- `24N.2.SL.TZ0.4` (examiner) — used final NaOH volume instead of equivalence volume
- `25N.1B.HL.6208.1` (markscheme) — volume used = final − initial; ECF for later marks
- `19M.2.HL.TZ1.5` (examiner) — no vertical drop; wrong equivalence location
- `18N.2.HL.TZ0.6` (examiner) — WB/WA curve features; equivalence at 25 cm3 missed
- `12M.2.HL.TZ1.6` (examiner) — pKa = pH at half-equivalence not related to initial pH
- `10M.2.HL.TZ2.7` (examiner) — half-equivalence: equal concentrations, maximum buffer capacity

## [ ] R3.1-T12 · Buffers: definition keywords, which component reacts, and the log-ratio shortcut
Priority 1 · R3.1.16, R3.1.17 · 44% correct (24N.1.HL.TZ0.26)

**1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge**
- *ao*: AO2–AO3
- *papers*:  - 1A
  - 2
- *syllabus hook*: Buffer pH depends on pKa and the ratio [A−]/[HA].
- *textbook gap*: The Henderson–Hasselbalch ratio direction and the reacting species are rarely practised under exam disguise.

**2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases**
- *trigger phrases*:  - resists change in pH
  - small amounts of acid or alkali
  - weak acid and its conjugate base
  - added H+ reacts with A−
  - added OH− reacts with HA
- *trap boundaries*:  - Omitting 'small' from the definition loses the mark (13M.2.HL.TZ1.1).
  - Not stating a WEAK acid (just 'acid') is insufficient (12N.2.HL.TZ0.3).
  - Equilibrium arrows in buffer-action equations only count with the direction of shift (25M.2.HL.6209.5).
  - Inverting [conjugate base]/[acid] in the calculation is the common error (17M.3.HL.TZ2.8, 19M.3.SL.TZ2.16).
- *delta*: Name both components, which one neutralizes added H+ / OH−, and the equation for each.

**3 · Novel application**
- *disguises*:  - Phosphate buffer (Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4) with strong base added
  - Buffer made by half-neutralizing a weak acid (2:1 acid:NaOH)
  - Buffer pH with unequal concentrations
- *heuristic*: Added OH− always reacts with the acid component; added H+ always reacts with the base component. Spectator Na+ never reacts.

**4 · Command terms & score mechanics**
- *terms*:  - Explain
  - Calculate
  - Describe
- *score mechanics*: Explain buffer action = one equation for added acid + one for added base.

**5 · Time efficiency**
- *workflow*:  - pH = pKa + log([A−]/[HA]) (memorised)
  - Ratio < 1 → pH below pKa; ratio > 1 → above
  - log 2 ≈ 0.30, log 0.5 ≈ −0.30
- *fat to trim*: In Paper 1A you rarely need a calculator: the sign of the log and ±0.30 settle it.

**6 · Data booklet**
- *sections*:  - —
- *hack*: The buffer equation pH = pKa + log([A−]/[HA]) is not in the data booklet: memorise it, or derive it from the Ka expression.

**7 · Instant elimination**
- *rules*:  - If [salt] < [acid], pH < pKa: eliminate every option at or above pKa.
  - 'OH− reacts with Na+' is always wrong: spectator ion.
  - Adding base to an H2PO4−/HPO4²− buffer increases [HPO4²−] and decreases [H2PO4−]: eliminate the reverse.

**Evidence**
- `25M.1A.HL.6219.35` (mcq) — methanoic acid/methanoate 2:1 → pH = 3.75 − 0.30 = 3.45
- `24N.1.HL.TZ0.26` (mcq) — 44% correct; which phosphate species increases on adding base
- `25M.2.HL.6209.5` (markscheme) — 2:1 acid:NaOH → half-equivalence; buffer equations; ⇌ needs the direction of shift
- `13M.2.HL.TZ1.1` (examiner) — omitted 'small'
- `12N.2.HL.TZ0.3` (examiner) — did not say 'weak' acid
- `11M.2.HL.TZ2.5` (examiner) — did not explain the specific NH3/NH4Cl buffer
- `17M.3.HL.TZ2.8` (examiner) — inverted [conjugate base] and [acid]
- `19M.3.SL.TZ2.16` (examiner) — inverted ratio

## [ ] R3.1-T02 · Strength is extent of ionization, not number of protons or concentration
Priority 2 · R3.1.6 · 51% correct (25M.1A.HL.6213.33)

**1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge**
- *ao*: AO1–AO3
- *papers*:  - 1A
  - 2
- *syllabus hook*: Strong and weak acids/bases differ in the extent of ionization.
- *textbook gap*: Diprotic and concentrated acids are often described as 'stronger' in everyday language; the exam rewards only the ionization argument.

**2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases**
- *trigger phrases*:  - ionizes/dissociates completely
  - partially ionizes/dissociates
  - extent/degree of ionization
- *trap boundaries*:  - 'Dissolves' instead of 'dissociates/ionizes' loses the mark (19M.2.HL.TZ2.5).
  - Explanations based on the number of acidic protons are marked wrong (24M.2.HL.TZ1.1, 14M.3.SL.TZ2.21).
- *delta*: Name the property (extent of ionization) AND the comparison (strong: complete; weak: partial).

**3 · Novel application**
- *disguises*:  - 'Why does sulfuric acid have a lower pH than methanoic acid of the same concentration?' with a 'diprotic' option
  - Two acids of equal concentration titrated with the same base
- *heuristic*: If the option is true but explains a different quantity (protons per molecule, solubility), it is a distractor.

**4 · Command terms & score mechanics**
- *terms*:  - Explain
  - Outline
- *score mechanics*: Explain = reason + consequence: 'ionizes more completely, so [H+] is higher, so pH is lower'.

**5 · Time efficiency**
- *workflow*:  - Spot equal concentrations in the stem
  - Answer with ionization
  - Ignore proton count unless moles are asked
- *fat to trim*: No calculation needed to rank equal-concentration acids by pH.

**6 · Data booklet**
- *sections*:  - —
- *hack*: —

**7 · Instant elimination**
- *rules*:  - 'Diprotic' explains moles of base needed, not why the acid is strong: eliminate it on 'why lower pH' questions.
  - Equal moles of HCl and CH3COOH need the SAME volume of NaOH to neutralize: any option that says the strong acid needs more is out.
  - 'More soluble' is never the reason an acid is strong.

**Evidence**
- `25M.1A.HL.6213.33` (mcq) — 51% correct; 'diprotic' distractor
- `25M.1A.SL.TZ2.26` (examiner) — 29% attributed lower pH to the acid being diprotic
- `24M.2.HL.TZ1.1` (examiner) — number of acidic protons confused with strength
- `24M.2.HL.TZ1.5` (examiner) — shifted the equivalence volume, confusing strength with concentration
- `13N.2.SL.TZ0.5` (examiner) — few saw that the same volume of base is needed
- `10N.2.SL.TZ0.6` (examiner) — same volume of acid needed was missed
- `19M.2.HL.TZ2.5` (examiner) — 'dissolve' instead of 'dissociate'

## [ ] R3.1-T11 · Choosing and explaining indicators: pKa ≈ pH at equivalence; HIn ⇌ H+ + In−
Priority 2 · R3.1.14, R3.1.15 · 54% correct (25M.1A.HL.6213.35)

**1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge**
- *ao*: AO2–AO3
- *papers*:  - 1A
  - 2
- *syllabus hook*: Indicators are weak acids whose conjugate forms differ in colour; the end-point range must sit on the equivalence pH.
- *textbook gap*: Phenolphthalein is the default in labs, so students choose it for weak-base titrations.

**2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases**
- *trigger phrases*:  - HIn ⇌ H+ + In−
  - conjugate acid and base forms have different colours
  - equivalence point falls within the pH range of the indicator
  - pKa in the middle of the range
- *trap boundaries*:  - 'Colourless in acid, pink in alkali' alone is not accepted as the explanation (24N.2.HL.TZ0.5).
  - Explanations without the indicator equilibrium lost credit (09N.2.HL.TZ0.8, 11M.2.HL.TZ1.8, 14N.2.HL.TZ0.8).
  - Stating only that the indicator 'changes colour' without the colours or the range did not score (12M.2.HL.TZ2.8, 16M.3.SL.TZ0.2).
- *delta*: Name the equilibrium, both coloured forms, and link the range to the equivalence pH.

**3 · Novel application**
- *disguises*:  - Phenylamine titrated with nitric acid
  - Excess ammonia added to HCl with a curve and an indicator table
  - Indicator colour in a coloured solution (absorbed vs observed colour)
- *heuristic*: Find the equivalence pH from the salt (T09) → pick the indicator with pKa closest to it (±1).

**4 · Command terms & score mechanics**
- *terms*:  - Explain
  - Identify
  - Justify
- *score mechanics*: Justify the choice = range overlaps the vertical section/equivalence pH.

**5 · Time efficiency**
- *workflow*:  - Strong/weak combination → equivalence pH
  - Data booklet section 18: pick pKa ≈ that pH
  - Justify in one line
- *fat to trim*: No calculation of the exact equivalence pH needed for Paper 1A.

**6 · Data booklet**
- *sections*:  - 18
- *hack*: Section 18 gives pKa and pH range for each indicator: weak base + strong acid → low-pKa indicators (bromophenol blue, bromocresol green, methyl orange).

**7 · Instant elimination**
- *rules*:  - Weak base + strong acid: eliminate phenolphthalein and phenol red (they change above 7).
  - Weak acid + strong base: eliminate methyl orange and bromophenol blue.
  - Strong + strong: most indicators work; choose the one whose range lies inside the vertical section.

**Evidence**

## [ ] R3.1-T04 · Conjugate pairs and amphiprotic species: differ by exactly one H+
Priority 3 · R3.1.1, R3.1.2, R3.1.3 · 66% correct (25M.1A.HL.6213.31)

**1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge**
- *ao*: AO1–AO2
- *papers*:  - 1A
  - 2
- *syllabus hook*: A conjugate pair differs by a single proton; some species act as both acid and base.
- *textbook gap*: Charge bookkeeping and drawing the conjugate base of an organic acid are rarely drilled but always marked.

**2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases**
- *trigger phrases*:  - proton/H+ donor
  - proton/H+ acceptor
  - differ by one proton/H+
- *trap boundaries*:  - Conjugate base drawn without all bonds/atoms, or with the negative charge misplaced, loses the mark (19M.2.SL.TZ1.2, 19M.2.HL.TZ1.2).
  - Wrong charge on the conjugate base (e.g. carbonate) loses the mark even when the species is right (19M.2.HL.TZ2.5).
  - Accept H+ in place of H3O+ (20N.2.HL.TZ0.5).
- *delta*: Correct species AND correct charge; for organic acids, only the –COOH hydrogen leaves.

**3 · Novel application**
- *disguises*:  - Phosphate series (H3PO4 / H2PO4− / HPO4²− / PO4³−)
  - Ammonia in an unfamiliar reaction: which equation shows it accepting a proton?
  - Alcohol + carboxylic acid in one molecule
- *heuristic*: Amphiprotic = has a removable H AND can take one more: always the middle members of a polyprotic series, plus H2O.

**4 · Command terms & score mechanics**
- *terms*:  - Identify
  - Deduce
  - State
- *score mechanics*: State/Identify: formula with charge is the whole mark; no explanation needed.

**5 · Time efficiency**
- *workflow*:  - Write the pair side by side
  - Count H and charge: Δ = one H, one unit of charge
  - Check direction (who loses the proton)
- *fat to trim*: Ignore spectator ions (Na+, Cl−) when finding pairs.

**6 · Data booklet**
- *sections*:  - —
- *hack*: —

**7 · Instant elimination**
- *rules*:  - A species with no H (PO4³−) cannot be a Brønsted–Lowry acid: any pair containing it as an acid is out.
  - 'Hydrogen atom' or 'electron pair' is never what converts a base into its conjugate acid: it is a proton.
  - In 'ammonia as a Brønsted–Lowry base', eliminate equations where NH3 loses H (forms NH2− or NO).

**Evidence**

## [ ] R3.1-T08 · pH of a weak acid or base: assumptions, Ka vs Kb, and the base two-step
Priority 3 · R3.1.10, R3.1.9 · repeatedly weak in Paper 2 reports 2010–2025

**1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge**
- *ao*: AO2
- *papers*:  - 2
- *syllabus hook*: Ka = [H+][A−]/[HA]; for weak species [HA]eq ≈ initial concentration.
- *textbook gap*: Worked examples are usually acids; exams increasingly ask for weak bases from pKb.

**2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases**
- *trigger phrases*:  - [H+] = [A−]
  - [HA] ≈ initial concentration
  - [OH−]² / c = Kb
  - pH = 14.00 − pOH
- *trap boundaries*:  - Weak-base answers below pH 7 earn no ECF (25M.2.HL.6221.3).
  - Confusing Ka with Kb, and not assuming [NH3]eq ≈ starting concentration or [NH4+] = [OH−], were the common failures (15M.2.HL.TZ2.7).
  - Not allowing for dilution gave impossibly high pH values (15M.2.HL.TZ2.7).
- *delta*: State both assumptions, compute [OH−] (base) or [H+] (acid), then convert.

**3 · Novel application**
- *disguises*:  - Ammonia given as a complexing ligand, then pH asked from pKb
  - Acid diluted before measuring
- *heuristic*: Base question → solve for [OH−] → pOH → pH. Never put Kb into an [H+] expression.

**4 · Command terms & score mechanics**
- *terms*:  - Calculate
  - Determine
- *score mechanics*: Three-mark pattern: K expression/value → concentration → pH; 'Award [3] for correct final answer'.

**5 · Time efficiency**
- *workflow*:  - K = 10^−pK
  - x = √(K × c)
  - pH (acid) or 14 − pOH (base)
  - Sanity check vs a strong species at the same concentration
- *fat to trim*: Skip the quadratic: IB accepts the small-x approximation.

**6 · Data booklet**
- *sections*:  - 1
  - 2
- *hack*: Section 1 gives pH, pOH and Kw = [H+][OH−]; section 2 gives Kw's value. Ka/Kb expressions and pK = −log K are not printed: memorise them.

**7 · Instant elimination**
- *rules*:  - A weak acid at concentration c has pH > −log c: eliminate options equal to the strong-acid pH.
  - 0.1 mol dm−3 of a weak base cannot reach pH 13: eliminate it.

**Evidence**

## [ ] R3.1-T07 · Ranking with Ka, Kb, pKa, pKb: the weaker the acid, the stronger its conjugate base
Priority 4 · R3.1.10, R3.1.11 · appears in both 2024 and 2025 Paper 1

**1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge**
- *ao*: AO2–AO3
- *papers*:  - 1A
  - 2
- *syllabus hook*: Ka × Kb = Kw and pKa + pKb = 14.00 at 298 K: both are not printed in the data booklet: memorise it.
- *textbook gap*: Tables mix K and pK columns; the direction flips between them.

**2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases**
- *trigger phrases*:  - higher pKa = weaker acid
  - lower Ka
  - pKa + pKb = 14
- *trap boundaries*:  - Reasoning without quoting the pKa or Ka values failed to score (14N.2.HL.TZ0.4).
- *delta*: Cite the actual values and state the inverse link explicitly.

**3 · Novel application**
- *disguises*:  - 'Which conjugate base is strongest?' given Ka values
  - 'Which base has the strongest conjugate acid?' given a mixed Kb/pKb table
- *heuristic*: Strongest conjugate base ← weakest acid (smallest Ka, largest pKa). Strongest conjugate acid ← weakest base (smallest Kb, largest pKb).

**4 · Command terms & score mechanics**
- *terms*:  - Deduce
  - Explain
  - Compare
- *score mechanics*: Compare: refer to both species with their values.

**5 · Time efficiency**
- *workflow*:  - Convert everything to one scale (all pK or all K)
  - Flip direction for conjugates
  - Pick the extreme
- *fat to trim*: No need to calculate conjugate K values; the ranking just inverts.

**6 · Data booklet**
- *sections*:  - 2
- *hack*: Only Kw's value is in the booklet (section 2). Ka/Kb/pKa tables come with the question; the Ka × Kb = Kw link is yours to remember.

**7 · Instant elimination**
- *rules*:  - When asked for the strongest conjugate base, eliminate the conjugate of the acid with the largest Ka first.
  - In a mixed table, a pKb of 14.5 means an extremely weak base, so its conjugate acid is the strongest.

**Evidence**
- `24N.1.HL.TZ0.25` (mcq) — strongest conjugate base from the smallest Ka (ClO−)

  - 2

  - systematic error
- *trap boundaries*:  - Errors in significant figures are not penalized unless the markscheme says so; ECF carries a wrong first value through (25N.1B.HL.6208.1).
  - Forgetting the aliquot scaling factor (e.g. 25 cm3 taken from 1 dm3) and using an anhydrous molar mass for a hydrate were common errors (16N.2.SL.TZ0.2).
  - A non-standardized NaOH solution gives a systematic error and lower accuracy, not lower precision (24N.1.HL.TZ0.40).
- *delta*: Every number in the chain must be traceable: titre → moles → ratio → scaling → answer.

**3 · Novel application**
- *disguises*:  - Solid NaOH weighed out: why must it be standardized?
  - pH probe vs indicator for the end point
  - Sulfuric acid with a 1:2 ratio hidden in the equation
- *heuristic*: Write the balanced equation first; the ratio hides there.

**4 · Command terms & score mechanics**
- *terms*:  - Calculate
  - Suggest
  - Evaluate
- *score mechanics*: 1B: 'Suggest' wants a specific practical reason (e.g. NaOH absorbs water/CO2 from air), not a generic 'human error'.

**5 · Time efficiency**
- *workflow*:  - Titre = final − initial
  - n = c × V (dm3)
  - Apply the ratio
  - Scale aliquot → whole sample
- *fat to trim*: Don't round mid-calculation; sig-fig slips are usually not penalized, arithmetic slips are.

**6 · Data booklet**
- *sections*:  - 1
  - 23
- *hack*: Section 1: n = cV; section 23: uncertainty rules for the evaluation parts.

**7 · Instant elimination**
- *rules*:  - Random error affects precision; systematic error affects accuracy: eliminate mismatched pairs.
  - If the acid is diprotic, the base volume doubles for the same moles: check options for a factor of 2.

**Evidence**
- `25N.1B.HL.6208.1` (markscheme) — titre from burette readings; ECF; sig figs not penalized; standardization of NaOH
- `24N.1.HL.TZ0.40` (mcq) — non-standardized NaOH → systematic error, lower accuracy

- `13N.2.SL.TZ0.5` (examiner) — diprotic sulfuric acid

- *papers*:  - 2
  - 1B

  - Base + H2O ⇌ BH+ + OH−
  - Check charges sum to zero
- *fat to trim*: State symbols are ignored unless the question asks for them (markscheme general rule).

**7 · Instant elimination**

## [ ] R3.1-T06 · Kw and temperature: pH of pure water falls when hot, but it stays neutral
Priority 9 · R3.1.5 · low frequency, high trap density

**1 · Syllabus-to-exam bridge**
- *ao*: AO2–AO3
- *papers*:  - 2
  - 1A
- *syllabus hook*: Kw = [H+][OH−]; water's ionization is endothermic.
- *textbook gap*: 'Neutral = pH 7' is only true at 298 K.

**2 · Markscheme logic & trigger phrases**
- *trigger phrases*:  - dissociation of water is endothermic
  - [H+] = [OH−] so still neutral
  - [H+] = √Kw
- *trap boundaries*:  - Saying pH does not change with temperature is wrong (10M.2.HL.TZ2.7).
  - The same number earned full credit whether labelled pH or pOH, because they are equal in pure water (24N.2.HL.TZ0.5).
  - Giving the value of Kw or Kw = Ka × Kb when the expression was asked loses the mark (10M.2.HL.TZ2.7).
- *delta*: Link endothermic → Kw increases → [H+] increases → pH decreases, AND state [H+] = [OH−].

**3 · Novel application**
- *disguises*:  - Kw given at 50 °C or 100 °C: calculate the pH of pure water
  - 'Is water at pH 6.5 acidic?'
- *heuristic*: Pure water: [H+] = √Kw at any temperature; neutrality means [H+] = [OH−], not pH 7.

**4 · Command terms & score mechanics**
- *terms*:  - Explain
  - Calculate
- *score mechanics*: Explain needs the endothermic step; Calculate needs √Kw then −log.

**5 · Time efficiency**
- *workflow*:  - [H+] = √Kw
  - pH = −log[H+]
  - Say 'neutral because [H+] = [OH−]'
- *fat to trim*: No ICE table needed for pure water.

**6 · Data booklet**
- *sections*:  - 1
  - 2
- *hack*: Kw at 298 K is in the booklet; other temperatures are always given in the question.

**7 · Instant elimination**
- *rules*:  - Any option calling hot pure water 'acidic' is wrong.
  - If Kw > 1.0 × 10^−14, the pH of pure water is below 7: eliminate pH ≥ 7 options.

**Evidence**
- `24N.2.HL.TZ0.5` (markscheme) — endothermic dissociation; pH = pOH = 6.51 accepted either way
- `10M.2.HL.TZ2.7` (examiner) — pH stated not to change; Kw value given instead of the expression
