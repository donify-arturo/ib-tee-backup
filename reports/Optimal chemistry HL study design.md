# Space, mix and earn chemistry mastery

The research supports one loop for one student: attempt each question unaided, get an explanation at once, come back to the same skill on a fresh past-paper question in at least three separate sessions, and do most practice in mixed sets where easily confused question types sit side by side. Retrieval with feedback and spacing are the secure parts of that loop, mixing is strong in maths and promising in chemistry, and the exact recipe (gap lengths, session counts, the Mastered rule) is inference, because no trial has tested a full system on 16-19-year-olds doing chemistry past papers. The student's app already gets the single card largely right: question first, help on demand, a reason beside each wrong option, and an explanation straight after answering. It falls short between cards, because it saves no attempts, never schedules a return, keeps each sub-topic separate, and lets a hand tick remove a card until the student unticks it. The changes worth making first are to log first attempts, schedule returns, mix R1.1 with R1.2, and make Mastered earned and expiring. A check of the code and decks also found two problems specific to the next exam: the R1.1 and R1.2 decks lack the I'm lost, Procedure and Tricks content, and all 15 R1.2 cards are one-mark multiple choice. The realistic expected gain is moderate and unmeasured, because the separately reported effect sizes overlap and cannot be added together.

## The best sequence is a loop across sessions, and no single study tested it

The order below is assembled from separate lines of evidence. It runs from a skill's first appearance to the point where it counts as known, and the strength column applies to this learner, not to the original study populations.

| Stage | What happens | Best anchor | Strength here |
|---|---|---|---|
| New skill | Worked example, then a small blocked run until one attempt works unaided | Stoichiometry tutor: no extra learning, but 21% less time ([Weitz et al. 2010](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/31r4s7c7)); structure helps low prior knowledge d = 0.505 and hurts high d = -0.428 ([secondary summary of Tetzlaff et al.](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/one-size-doesnt-fit-all-teaching-experts-and-novices/)) | Moderate |
| Attempt | Commit to an answer before anything is revealed | Same-content benefit g = 0.54, with required guessing 0.65 vs 0.22 ([St. Hilaire et al.](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-023-02353-8)) | Moderate; not chemistry |
| Feedback | Explanation immediately, aimed at the error | Right or wrong 0.05, correct answer 0.32, elaborated 0.49 ([Van der Kleij et al. 2015](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/86y84/effects-of-feedback-in-a-computer-based-learning-environment-on-students-learning-outcomes-a-meta-analysis)); timing effect 0.002 ([ManyClasses 1](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/dspace/items/bbf719db-91fe-4a2b-9c3b-dbf59f812bd3)) | Moderate to strong for direction, not size |
| Return | Same skill, fresh question, at least 3 separate sessions | Spaced vs massed retrieval g = 0.74 ([Latimier et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8)); 3 + 3 relearning rule ([Rawson & Dunlosky 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023956)) | Strong for facts, inferred for multi-step chemistry |
| Mix | Confusable types adjacent, labels hidden | Grade 7 maths RCT: 61% vs 38%, d = 0.83 ([Rohrer et al.](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2019-rohrer.pdf)); uncategorised chemistry homework higher on all tests, significant on two of five ([Cox & Gulacar](https://doi.org/10.1515/cti-2024-0083)) | Strong in maths, moderate in chemistry |
| Earn | Mastered only after delayed, unaided, varied success, then a later check | Students who dropped items once recalled retained .35 vs .78 for continued retrieval, with near-identical confidence ([Karpicke 2009](https://pcllab.github.io/downloads/2009/2009_Karpicke_JEPGeneral.pdf)) | Mechanism strong, rule untested |

Two design decisions follow from this. **Schedule the skill, not the question.** A past-paper question can be seen only a few times before the answer is memorised, Rohrer's trial never repeated a problem, and the fitted algorithms were trained on repeated flashcards. The FSRS benchmark also shows a zero-parameter moving average predicting about as well as FSRS-7 on log loss ([srs-benchmark](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark)), and no head-to-head trial of any algorithm on problem solving was found. A simple Leitner-style state per question type, drawing a new question at each return, is enough. FSRS or Leitner remains defensible for a small flashcard layer of pure recall, such as definitions.

**Set gaps from days to the exam, and keep them simple.** Optimal single-review gaps were about 3 days for a 7-day test, 8 days for 35 days and 12 days for 70 days, and the optimal gap gave 64% more recall than no gap ([Cepeda et al. 2008](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)). Expanding and equal schedules do not differ (g = 0.034, [Latimier et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8)), so an elaborate ladder adds nothing. When time runs short, cut initial repetitions before cutting a later session, because relearning sessions matter more than the initial criterion ([Rawson & Dunlosky 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023956)).

## Retrieval and spacing are secure, while the chemistry recipe is inferred

The notes support a clear split between what is established, what holds with limits, what is contested, and what is design judgement.

| Status | Claim | Evidence | Main limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Well supported | Retrieval with feedback beats restudy | Classroom g = 0.499, and 0.33 against restudy ([Yang et al. 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)); feedback 0.73 vs 0.39, and only 0.03 with no feedback at 50% success or lower ([Rowland 2014](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf)) | Chemistry g = 0.34, k = 10; subject was not a significant moderator |
| Well supported | Spacing beats massing, and the gap scales with the delay | [Cepeda et al. 2008](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf); Year 7 maths d = 0.61 and 0.39 ([Emeny et al. 2021](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2021-emeny.pdf)) | Mostly fact recall; no chemistry optimum exists |
| Well supported | Students misjudge their own learning | Massed practice overconfidence d = 0.92 and 1.07, spaced d = 0.15 and 0.23 ([Emeny et al. 2021](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2021-emeny.pdf)); answer-visible judgements inflate ([Koriat & Bjork 2005](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2005-koriat.pdf)) | Largely verbal materials or younger students |
| Supported, with limits | Mixing improves delayed problem solving | [Rohrer et al.](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2019-rohrer.pdf); maths meta-analysis g = 0.34 ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)); physics d = 0.40 and 0.91 on surprise tests, no significant midterm gain ([Samani & Pan 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8589969/)) | No 16-19 chemistry RCT; takes longer per problem |
| Supported, with limits | Guidance should fall as skill rises | [Tetzlaff summary](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/one-size-doesnt-fit-all-teaching-experts-and-novices/); adaptive fading beat fixed ([Salden et al. 2010](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11251-009-9107-8)) | Secondary source; no switching threshold known |
| Contested | Retrieval transfers to multi-step problems | Problem-solving d = 0.29, p = .10, worked-example studies near zero ([Pan & Rickard 2018](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PR_2018.pdf)); disputed by [Karpicke & Aue 2015](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-015-9309-3) | The null studies were lab-based and generally gave no feedback |
| Contested | "Why" prompts help | Science g = 0.565, high school 0.426 ([Bisra et al. 2018](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf)); negative moderator ([Barbieri et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1)); why-type questions hurt in general chemistry ([Pyburn et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1021/ed4009045)) | Different tasks and comparisons |
| Contested | Multiple choice is as good as written recall | Yang MC g = 0.567; cued recall 0.61 vs recognition 0.29 in Rowland; short answer only in [Greving & Richter 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/pdf); MC only in Pyburn | Unresolved, so practise both |
| Contested | Spacing pays in real chemistry courses | Small or non-significant in most of nine STEM courses ([Bego et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40594-024-00468-5)) | Quiz design may explain it |
| Inferred only | Skill-level scheduling, percentage gaps, 3-session Mastered rule, confusable pairs, help ladder, sure/unsure tap, redesigned Eliminate, session splits | Assembled in the notes from the studies above | Each is plausible, and none is tested as a package |
| Thin or absent | Past-paper practice itself; teaching tricks; test-wiseness | Self-grading g = 0.34 ([Sanchez et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000190)) is the nearest; no pooled trick effect, and chemistry heuristics often mislead ([Maeyer & Talanquer 2010](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/sce.20397)) | No IB study found |

Source quality deserves weight here. **No chemistry-specific paper in the notes was read in full text**, and several key figures come from abstracts or summaries, including Tetzlaff, Latimier and Cox & Gulacar. The notes also disagree on McLaren's stoichiometry result: one reading finds equal learning and the other finds more conceptual learning from examples and more procedural learning from tutoring. Both agree on the time saving of about 21%, so only that figure is used here. A headline of g = 0.87 for attempting before instruction ([Sinha & Kapur 2021](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105)) was left out because its status as a bias-corrected upper estimate could not be confirmed. Reviews after 2022 were not systematically searched, and May 2026 IB reports may exist that the local files lack.

The headline numbers also shrink on inspection. Rohrer's d = 0.83 already bundles mixing, spacing and retrieval in one package, and the authors note it may be smaller per minute and at short delays, with d = 0.42 at 1 day against 0.79 at 30 days in an earlier study ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)). An exam very close to now will therefore show less of the benefit than the final IB sitting will.

## The card follows the research, and the gaps sit between cards

I read `card_ui.py`, `app.py` and the decks to test the description given for this comparison, and the single card holds up well. Its question-first order fits the attempt evidence, and hiding the title protects strategy choice. Reasons appear beside the wrong option, with 29 of 30 possible reasons in R1.1 and 45 of 45 in R1.2, which is contiguity (g = 0.63, [Noetel et al. 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543211052329), measured on multimedia lessons). The answer and key idea show at once, which is the elaborated feedback the evidence favours, and on-demand help beat proactive help in the one small trial found (ES 0.35 [0.02, 0.74], [Razzaq & Heffernan 2010](https://web.cs.wpi.edu/~nth/pubs_and_grants/papers/2010/ITS/Razzaq%20Hints%20Is%20It%20Better%20to%20Give%20or%20Wait.pdf)).

| Feature | What the app does now | What the evidence implies | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commitment | Wrong taps stay marked, but "Tap an answer, or show it" reveals without an attempt | Required guessing is the actionable moderator | Close the bypass; route "never seen this" to help |
| Help buttons | Five equal chips plus I'm lost; Time and Data booklet show their content on the label | Designer-set order g = 0.41 vs learner-set 0.20 ([Noetel et al. 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543211052329)) | One Help button that steps through a ladder |
| Eliminate | Crosses out one option per tap and gives the rule | Discriminating between lures is what helps (d = 0.43, [Little & Bjork 2014](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2017/01/LittleBjorkMC2014.pdf)), so the button may do the student's work | Ask the student to rule one out first, then check |
| After answering | Answer and key idea, then optional Markscheme and Syllabus, plus method | Do not make the explanation a choice among equal tabs | Keep; open the method by default after errors only |
| Tricks | Shown after the answer, where a deck has them | Heuristics without boundaries become the error | Add a "fails when" line |
| Written cards | "Show the answer" only, with no self-marking | Self-scoring beat seeing the key ([Balch 1998](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15328023top2503_3)) | Tap each mark point earned |
| Queue | Fixed order by priority then ID, one sub-topic per page | Mixed sets; learners given control tend to mass and drop | App picks the next card |
| Review | None; a ticked card leaves the queue until unticked | Dropping after success .35 vs .78 ([Karpicke 2009](https://pcllab.github.io/downloads/2009/2009_Karpicke_JEPGeneral.pdf)) | Largest gap |
| Mastered | Hand tick | Judgement is unreliable straight after success | Earned, dated, can lapse |
| Notes page and "Things to remember" | Saved steps to read; the expander sits above the queue and lists every question type with its steps | Rereading is low utility ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)); self-explaining beat provided explanations, g = 0.354 ([Bisra et al. 2018](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf)) | Hide steps until recalled; move the expander out of the study queue |

Three facts from the code change the priorities. **Nothing about an attempt is stored**: taps stay inside a one-way HTML component, and only the Mastered tick and notes reach disk, so the app cannot tell a first-try success from a fifth-try one or from a hint-assisted one. **The I'm lost, Procedure and Tricks features are absent from both next-exam decks**: 0 of the 30 R1.1 and R1.2 cards have them, and they show the older "Remember" list instead, because only the 6 S1.2 cards were built with the newer fields. **Moving to an earned Mastered costs nothing today**: `data/progress.json` holds one entry, marked not mastered, so no real history is lost, and the attempt log can be a new file that leaves it untouched as the project rules require.

## R1.1 and R1.2 lose marks on skills the decks barely rehearse

Examiner evidence shows where marks go on these two sub-topics, and the numbers are specific. The [local subject reports](file:///C:/Users/Arturo/Documents/ib_tee/data/chunks/subject_reports_2001_2025.jsonl) were checked directly for the sign, bond-enthalpy, cycle and Paper 1B figures below, which match the report text; the other rows come from the research notes. Mean correct is 57.1% over 22 R1.1 items and 69.9% over 19 R1.2 items, though those figures come mostly from questions the examiners chose to discuss, which tend to be the harder ones.

| Exam failure | Examiner evidence | Practice move (inferred) |
|---|---|---|
| Right number, wrong sign | 24M.1.SL.TZ2.15: 39% correct, 37% chose the wrong-sign option; 25M.1A.HL.TZ2.19: 66% correct, 23% right value without the negative | Compulsory one-tap sign and unit check on every energetics calculation |
| Wrong mass or unit in Q = mcΔT | 25M.2.HL.TZ3.9: about half made mistakes, including using the solute's mass; mean 0.9/2 | Pair "which mass?" with "which moles?" |
| Bond enthalpy direction and counting | 25M.2.HL.TZ3.9: about 40% full marks, others miscounted bonds or subtracted the wrong way | Put next to ΔHf questions, whose direction reverses |
| Cycle not drawn or not recognised | 15M.2.HL.TZ1.6: those who did not draw the cycle tended to go wrong; 09M.2.SL.TZ1.2: unfamiliar layout not recognised | Sketch first; vary layouts between triangle, ladder and algebra |
| Definitions | R1.2 tally: definition, gaseous or average is the largest error group (24 sentences, overlapping categories, none after 2019) | Small recall-only flashcard layer |
| Practical thinking on Paper 1B | First sitting mean just over 15/35, under 43%, across just under 6,000 students; R1.1's largest error group is heat loss and practical (17) | Calorimetry as an experiment: error sources, heat-loss fixes, graph extrapolation |

The R1.2 deck does not match this picture. **All 15 R1.2 cards are one-mark multiple choice**, even though the deck tags 14 of them as Paper 2 techniques. Written marks are where bond-enthalpy working, ECF and definitions are examined, and Paper 2 carries 44% of the grade by a third-party summary ([Colourful Solutions](https://ibchem.com/IB25/assess.php); the official brief could not be opened). R1.1 is better balanced at 10 multiple-choice and 5 written cards, but 10 of its 15 cards are labelled calorimetry, so a fixed run through it is close to blocked. Two of its Paper 1B-relevant cards (R1.1-11 and R1.1-12) are flagged in `validation_issues` as having reconstructed question text, so they need checking against the real papers.

The conceptual gap needs attention too. Students solve chemistry calculations more often than paired conceptual questions ([Cracolice et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1021/ed085p873)), so a Mastered status earned from calculations alone overstates understanding. Each main calculation type should have a conceptual twin. The obvious targets include bond breaking, which over 80% of students in one introductory physical chemistry course treated as the source of a combustion's exothermicity ([Galley 2004](https://doi.org/10.1021/ed081p523)). Short refutation notes have general support (g = 0.41, [Schroeder & Kucera 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-021-09656-z)), and the closest bond-energy intervention worked by eliciting and repairing students' ideas ([Teichert & Stacy 2002](https://doi.org/10.1002/tea.10033)). No rigorous study of teaching Hess or Born-Haber cycles at this age was found, so the cycle advice rests on examiner remarks and working-memory logic.

The raw material for all of this already exists. The evidence packs hold 55 R1.1 and 58 R1.2 questions with both question text and markscheme. Every card already names follow-up questions in `try_next`, 31 and 33 of which are in the packs, though the app displays them only as plain references and their markscheme coverage still needs checking. Those references are the cheapest source of fresh variants for returns.

## Six changes come first, and the interface can stay at three taps

The order below reflects dependency first and then evidence strength. Items 1 to 3 together are the smallest version that changes learning, and items 5 and 6 are also the ones to bring forward if the exam leaves little time to build.

| # | Change | What the student sees | Support | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Log every first attempt: card, question, date, correct on first tap, help used, sure or unsure | One Sure / Unsure tap | Foundation; the data use is inferred, while hypercorrection supports flagging confident errors ([2011 study](https://typeset.io/pdf/the-hypercorrection-effect-persists-over-a-week-but-high-3hxdkh7nbu.pdf)) | Medium, as the card needs a two-way component |
| 2 | Schedule returns by question type, with a fresh question each time | "Due today: 12" | Strong principle, inferred implementation | Medium |
| 3 | Mixed session across R1.1 and R1.2, confusable types adjacent, one card at a time | Start button, no topic label | Moderate; contrast appears to need adjacency (g = 0.73 vs 0.22, inductive-learning studies, [Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)) | Small after item 2 |
| 4 | Earned Mastered: unaided success in 3 sessions, the last after a real gap, at least one unseen variant, at least one in a mixed set; failure returns it to Practising | "Mastered, check on 20 Nov" | Inferred rule from strong mechanisms | Small after item 1 |
| 5 | Close the show-answer bypass, merge help into a ladder, rebuild R1.1 and R1.2 with the newer fields using `--backend claude-cli` | Fewer buttons | Moderate; the ladder order is judgement | Small to medium |
| 6 | Add written and Paper 1B-style R1 cards, conceptual twins, the sign and unit check and a few refutation notes | Same card | Strong on examiner evidence, weak on intervention evidence | Medium |

The interface can get simpler while doing more: tap an answer, tap Sure or Unsure, read one short explanation, and move on. A why prompt appears only after an error or an unsure correct answer, as a typed line about the chemistry and never as "how confident are you?", since metacognitive prompts are the weakest type (g = 0.192, k = 3, [Bisra et al. 2018](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf)). On calculation cards, hide the options until the student has a number, which matches the examiners' advice to [solve maths questions as short answers](file:///C:/Users/Arturo/Documents/ib_tee/data/chunks/subject_reports_2001_2025.jsonl). Progress should count only delayed first-try results, because mixed practice lowers same-day accuracy (0.69 vs 0.74 and 0.67 vs 0.76, [Samani & Pan 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8589969/)). A single line explaining that this is expected would stop the student reading it as failure.

**The exam date is not recorded**, and it sets everything else, so the app needs one input for it. It also matters whether "examined next" means a school test or a formal session. The table is a judgement scaled from the fact-recall gaps above, not measured chemistry optima.

| Days to exam | Gap between returns | Returns per question type | New skills |
|---|---|---|---|
| About 7 | About 3 days | 2 to 3 | Triage only |
| About 14 | 3 to 5 days | 3 | Triage only |
| About 35 | About 8 days | 3 to 4 | About 10% of time |
| About 70 | About 12 days | 3 to 4 | About 30% of time |

Before a topic exam, give every question type two spaced attempts before any type gets a fourth. If the date is too close to build anything, the same plan works by hand: mix the two decks' `try_next` questions, attempt closed-book, mark against the markscheme and repeat the wrong types on another day. Save the few genuine 2025 Paper 1B questions for timed use and avoid spending them on early learning.

Several things are not worth building now. Per-question FSRS is out for the reasons above. Animations have small effects (g = 0.23, tabulated in [Noetel et al. 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543211052329)), and the one comparison found favoured generating drawings over more simulation use ([Zhang & Linn 2011](https://doi.org/10.1002/tea.20443)). Why prompts on every card cost time against conflicting evidence, and test-form tricks such as "the longest option" have no support here, so elimination should stay grounded in chemistry. The main risk in this plan is unstudied: a self-chooser who dislikes friction may stop using the app, and no effect size captures that, so friction should be added one tap at a time and dropped if use falls.

## Conclusion

The optimisation target turns out to be different from the one the app was built around. Most of the work so far has gone into making each card richer, yet the evidence says most of the remaining gain lies in when a card returns and what sits next to it, and the app cannot act on either without first remembering what happened. A plain attempt log is therefore the single most valuable feature, even though no study measures it, because every well-supported effect depends on it. Two horizons also need separating: gaps tuned to a nearby R1 test are too short for the final IB sitting, so once the test passes, R1 skills should move to gaps of roughly two to three weeks and not retire.

The bigger unknown is whether this package works for chemistry at this age at all, and the student's own log can give a partial answer that the literature cannot. Comparing first-try accuracy on fresh twin questions after a gap, for skills practised with and without a feature, tests the design directly, though one learner's data will reveal only large differences. Until then, the honest claim is that the plan aligns the app with the best-supported principles, and that its size of benefit for this student remains unmeasured.
